# Compliance Framework, Governance and Risk Assessment

*Knowledge area: Compliance framework and governance. Audience: compliance managers at Indian banks and NBFCs. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation against current RBI directions and circulars.*

## 1. What compliance risk is

Compliance risk is the risk of legal or regulatory sanctions, financial loss or reputational damage that an institution suffers when it fails to comply with laws, regulations, codes of conduct and internal policies. For an Indian bank or NBFC the obligations come from many places: the RBI Act and Banking Regulation Act, RBI Master Directions and circulars, the Prevention of Money Laundering Act (PMLA), FEMA, SEBI and IRDAI rules where the entity distributes or sells those products, the Companies Act, and data protection law.

Compliance is not a department that "approves" things. Its job is to identify obligations, advise the business on how to meet them, test whether they are being met, and report honestly when they are not. The business owns compliance; the compliance function provides oversight and challenge.

## 2. The three lines of defence

| Line | Who | Role |
|---|---|---|
| **First** | Business units and operations (branches, lending, collections, product teams) | Own the risk and the controls. Day-to-day compliance sits here. |
| **Second** | Compliance, risk management | Set policy, advise, monitor, test and report independently of the business. |
| **Third** | Internal audit | Independent assurance to the audit committee on whether the first two lines work. |

Practical points:

- A common failure is the first line treating compliance as the owner of a breach ("compliance did not tell us"). Coach business heads to own their controls.
- Compliance must not drift into doing the business's checks (for example approving individual KYC files as a routine). That blurs independence and removes accountability from the first line.
- Internal audit looks back at what happened; compliance looks forward and in real time.

## 3. The Chief Compliance Officer and the compliance function

RBI has set out expectations for the compliance function in banks (the 2007 guidance on compliance function, and later directions) and for NBFCs through the scale-based regulation framework, which requires a Chief Compliance Officer (CCO) for certain layers. [Verify: the current RBI direction on CCO appointment, eligibility, tenure, reporting line and the NBFC layers to which it applies.]

Core expectations of the CCO and function, as typically stated:

- Independence from business lines, with direct access to the board or its audit committee.
- A reporting line that avoids conflict, for example not reporting into a revenue head. [Verify: the specific reporting line required.]
- Adequate resources, skills and authority to challenge.
- Not carrying a role that conflicts with compliance, such as business targets.
- An approved compliance policy, reviewed periodically, and an annual compliance plan.

## 4. Compliance risk assessment

A compliance risk assessment (CRA) answers the question "where are we most likely to breach, and how bad would it be?" A workable method:

1. **Build an obligations register.** List each regulatory requirement, the source (circular, section), the owner, the frequency and the control. Without a register you cannot assess anything.
2. **Score inherent risk.** For each obligation, score likelihood of breach and impact (penalty, supervisory action, customer harm, reputational damage). Consider volume, complexity, manual versus automated processes and the history of breaches.
3. **Assess control effectiveness.** Are controls designed properly and operating? Use testing results, audit findings, incident data and complaints.
4. **Derive residual risk.** Inherent risk reduced by control strength. Rate high, medium or low.
5. **Prioritise.** High residual risks get deeper testing, action plans and board visibility.
6. **Refresh.** Review at least annually and on triggers such as new products, new regulation, a major incident or an RBI observation.

Useful inputs: RBI inspection reports, penalty history across the industry, internal audit reports, customer complaint themes, whistle-blower reports, near misses, and key risk indicators.

## 5. Compliance testing and monitoring

Testing is where compliance proves whether controls work. Typical elements:

- **Annual compliance testing plan**, risk-based, approved by the CCO and noted by the audit committee.
- **Test design.** Define the control, the test steps, sample size, population and evidence. Sample sizes should be justified, not arbitrary.
- **Thematic reviews** (for example all digital lending partners, or all branches' handling of cash deposits above reporting limits) as opposed to only branch-by-branch checks.
- **Exceptions and issue management.** Each finding has an owner, a root cause, a due date and a closure check. Track ageing. Repeat findings should escalate.
- **Key risk and compliance indicators**, such as delayed regulatory returns, overdue KYC refreshes, complaints not closed within TAT, and training completion.

## 6. Reporting to senior management and the board

The audit committee of the board (and the risk committee where relevant) should receive a periodic compliance report. Good content:

- Summary rating of overall compliance risk and the direction of travel.
- Material breaches, penalties and regulatory communications.
- Regulatory changes in flight and any that will miss their deadline.
- Testing results and overdue actions, with owners.
- Emerging risks and resource gaps.

Keep it decision-oriented: what the committee must know, what it must decide, and what you need from management. Avoid thick appendices of unread tables.

## 7. Worked example: weak control found in a new product

A mid-sized NBFC launches a digital personal loan product through a lending service provider (LSP). Six weeks after launch, a compliance thematic review finds:

- The key fact statement (KFS) shown on the app does not include the annual percentage rate (APR) in one version of the screen flow.
- Recovery agent calls are made by the LSP without call recordings being retained.
- Grievance contact details are not visible on the loan agreement.

Compliance response:

1. Classify severity. The KFS gap is a customer-disclosure breach affecting every borrower, so it is high. The others are medium to high.
2. Inform the business head and the CCO immediately; do not wait for the quarterly report. Ask for the screen flow to be fixed within days. [Verify: the applicable RBI digital lending and KFS direction requirements.]
3. Quantify the exposure: how many customers onboarded, over what period, and what remediation is needed (for example re-issuing the KFS).
4. Agree a time-bound action plan with named owners and a retest date.
5. Report to the audit committee at the next meeting, including whether a self-disclosure to RBI is needed. [Verify: reporting expectations for material non-compliance.]
6. Add a pre-launch compliance sign-off to the new-product approval process so this does not recur.

## 8. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Treating the compliance policy as a document to file rather than a living control.
- Having an obligations register that is not updated when circulars change.
- Reporting only counts of issues rather than risk and trend.
- Allowing compliance to be consulted too late in product design; insist on being in the room before launch.
- Not escalating repeat findings or ageing actions.
- Being seen as "the department of no". Offer options: "Here is how you can do this compliantly."
- Confusing audit with compliance testing; they are separate and complementary.

## 9. Practice questions

1. Why should compliance not approve individual KYC files as a routine activity?
   *(It makes compliance part of first-line operations, weakens independence and lets the business off the hook for owning its controls. Compliance should test and oversee a sample.)*
2. What is the difference between inherent and residual compliance risk?
   *(Inherent is the risk before considering controls; residual is what remains after taking control effectiveness into account.)*
3. A new product is about to launch and compliance was not consulted. What do you do?
   *(Escalate to the CCO and product head, review the product against regulatory requirements, set conditions for launch or defer it, and strengthen the product approval process so compliance sign-off is mandatory.)*
