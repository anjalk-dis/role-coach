# Conduct, Regulatory Change and Inspections

*Knowledge area: Conduct risk, regulatory change management and handling RBI inspections. Audience: compliance managers at Indian banks and NBFCs. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation against current RBI circulars and directions.*

## 1. Conduct risk and fair practices

Conduct risk is the risk that the institution's behaviour, products or sales practices cause unfair outcomes for customers. In India, RBI has pushed strongly on customer protection through:

- **Fair Practices Code** for NBFCs and the Code of Conduct and customer protection norms for banks. Core themes: clear loan terms in the customer's language, transparency on charges and interest, fair recovery practices, no coercive collection, and fair handling of complaints. [Verify: the current Fair Practices Code provisions and applicable master directions.]
- **Digital lending directions**: disclosure through a key fact statement (KFS), APR, disbursement and repayment directly between the borrower's and the lender's accounts, a cooling-off period, and rules on lending service providers and data. [Verify: current direction text and details.]
- **Interest rate and charges transparency**, including rules on penal charges. [Verify: current rules on penal charges and floating rate reset practices.]
- **Customer service and liability** for unauthorised electronic transactions, and limits on customer liability under RBI instructions. [Verify.]

Compliance's role: embed these requirements into product design, sales scripts, collections processes and vendor oversight, then test them.

## 2. Grievance redress

Customer complaints are an early warning signal for compliance risk. Key elements of a sound redress framework:

- A Board-approved grievance redress policy with clear turnaround times and escalation levels.
- Visible contact points: nodal officer and principal nodal officer, with details on the website, in branches and on loan documents.
- Complaints logged, categorised, root-caused and closed within TAT. [Verify: RBI prescribed TAT and compensation for delays, for example for complaints not resolved within the prescribed period.]
- Awareness of the **RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme** and the process for escalation. [Verify: current scheme and timelines.]
- Analysis of complaint themes (for example loan foreclosure charges, unexplained debits, recovery agent behaviour) to drive product or process fixes.

A rising complaint trend with the same root cause is a compliance finding, not just a customer-service problem.

## 3. Mis-selling

Mis-selling happens when a product is sold to a customer for whom it is unsuitable, or with misleading information. In BFSI the classic cases are third-party products sold at the branch: insurance bundled with loans or deposits, mutual funds, and structured products. Red flags:

- Sudden spikes in sales of a single product to elderly customers or to customers with low income.
- Sales to customers who believe the product is a fixed deposit.
- Insurance premiums added to the loan without explicit informed consent.
- Sales incentives that reward volumes and ignore suitability or later complaints.
- High early surrender or free-look cancellation rates.

Controls: suitability assessment, scripts and call recording, pre-sales disclosure, post-sales verification calls, incentive design that includes quality metrics, and mystery shopping. [Verify: current RBI guidance on sale of third-party products and bancassurance.]

## 4. Regulatory change management

India's regulators issue a steady flow of circulars, master directions and FAQs. A good change process:

1. **Capture.** Monitor RBI, SEBI, IRDAI, FIU-IND, MCA and other sources daily. Log each item.
2. **Triage.** Is it relevant? Is it new, an amendment or a clarification? What is the effective date?
3. **Impact assessment.** Which policies, processes, systems, products, vendors and customer communications are affected? Involve business, technology, legal and operations.
4. **Gap analysis and action plan.** Define what to change, owner and date. Include technology build time, which is often the bottleneck.
5. **Implement and sign off.** Update policy and procedure documents, train staff, and test.
6. **Verify and close.** Compliance tests that the change works as intended. Update the obligations register.
7. **Report.** Show the audit committee the pipeline, with items at risk of missing deadlines.

If an effective date cannot be met, raise it early and consider seeking clarification or extension from RBI through proper channels, backed by an interim control plan. Do not leave it until the date has passed.

## 5. Handling RBI inspections and observations

RBI conducts supervisory assessments such as inspections under the supervisory framework (for banks and larger NBFCs) and may issue an inspection report with findings, risk assessments and directions. [Verify: the current supervisory framework and terminology.]

Good practice:

- **Preparation.** Keep a ready repository of policies, board and committee minutes, returns, audit reports and previous compliance statuses. Know the previous report's open points.
- **During the inspection.** Single point of contact, a log of all data requests and responses, and consistent factual answers. Do not speculate or guess; say you will revert with evidence.
- **Observations.** Read each one carefully, understand what exactly is alleged, and separate factual accuracy from disagreement on interpretation.
- **Response.** Acknowledge genuine gaps, give root cause, corrective action, owner and timeline, and preventive action. Where you disagree, say so respectfully with evidence. Seek board or committee approval for the response where required.
- **Tracking.** Maintain a closure tracker. Repeat observations from earlier inspections are viewed seriously.
- **Penalties.** RBI can impose monetary penalties and other supervisory action for contraventions. Understand the facts, prepare the show-cause response and consider disclosure obligations. [Verify: current penalty regime, enforcement framework and disclosure requirements.]

## 6. Worked example: responding to an observation

An RBI inspection of an NBFC notes: "Interest on certain loans was charged from the date of sanction rather than the date of disbursal, resulting in excess charges to borrowers." Operations believes it is a small issue limited to a few accounts.

Compliance approach:

1. Verify the facts: run a query across the loan system for all loans disbursed in the last two years where interest start date differs from the disbursal date. Suppose it finds 3,200 loans with a total excess charge of about Rs 18 lakh.
2. Root cause: a configuration in the loan management system defaulted to sanction date for a particular product variant, and no reconciliation control existed.
3. Corrective action: fix the configuration, refund the excess interest with any applicable adjustments to affected borrowers, and send a clear communication.
4. Preventive action: add a monthly automated reconciliation and a compliance test of the interest start date field in new product configuration.
5. Response: send RBI a factual response with numbers, root cause, completed actions and a closure date. Do not minimise the number. Inform the audit committee.
6. Track to closure and retest after three months.

The strongest responses are those where the institution found the full extent before RBI asked, and already refunded customers.

## 7. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Treating a circular as "operations' problem" and never testing its implementation.
- Reading only the headline of a master direction and missing a sub-clause.
- Answering an observation defensively or narrowly, which invites further scrutiny.
- Under-quantifying customer impact.
- Not involving technology early; many changes need system builds.
- Letting incentive schemes drive mis-selling and then blaming the sales staff.
- Treating complaints as isolated rather than as data.
- Sending the same weak action plan for a repeat observation.

## 8. Practice questions

1. A new RBI circular is effective in 90 days, but the system change will take 150 days. What do you do?
   *(Raise it immediately with senior management, define an interim manual control, document the plan and communicate or seek clarification from RBI through the right channel. Do not wait until the deadline.)*
2. What makes an RBI observation response strong?
   *(Accurate facts and full quantification, honest root cause, corrective and preventive actions with owners and dates, evidence, and a closure tracker.)*
3. Complaints about a loan foreclosure fee double in two months. Is that a compliance matter?
   *(Yes. It is an early signal of a disclosure or fair practices issue. Root-cause it, check disclosures and charges against regulatory rules, remediate customers and fix the process.)*
