# Credit Appraisal, Credit Policy and Risk Appetite

*Knowledge area: Credit appraisal, policy and risk appetite. Audience: credit risk managers. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. The credit lifecycle

1. **Sourcing:** the business originates the proposal.
2. **Due diligence and KYC:** identity, ownership, beneficial owners, AML checks, bureau and fraud checks.
3. **Appraisal:** financial and non-financial assessment of repayment capacity and risk.
4. **Rating and pricing:** internal rating assigned, and a risk-based price that covers cost of funds, operating cost, expected loss and a return on the capital consumed.
5. **Sanction:** approval by the authority set out in the **delegation of financial powers (DoFP)**. Larger or riskier exposures go to higher committees.
6. **Documentation and security creation:** loan agreements, charges on collateral, guarantees.
7. **Disbursement:** after conditions precedent are met.
8. **Monitoring and review:** covenant tracking, annual reviews, early warning signals.
9. **Collections, recovery or exit.**

The credit risk manager's job is to make sure each step is **independent, documented and consistent with policy**, not to do the business's job for it.

## 2. The 5 Cs of credit

| C | Question | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Character** | Will they pay? | Track record, bureau history, promoter integrity, past defaults or restructurings |
| **Capacity** | Can they pay? | Cash flows, DSCR, income stability, FOIR for retail |
| **Capital** | How much skin in the game? | Net worth, leverage, promoter contribution |
| **Collateral** | What's our fallback? | Security type, value, enforceability, LTV |
| **Conditions** | What's happening around them? | Industry cycle, regulation, macro environment, purpose of the loan |

**Key principle:** lend on **cash flow, not collateral**. Collateral reduces loss *if* default happens (LGD). It doesn't reduce the *chance* of default (PD). A loan that depends on selling the collateral to be repaid is a weak loan.

## 3. Key financial ratios

**Corporate / SME:**
- **DSCR (Debt Service Coverage Ratio)** = cash available for debt service ÷ (principal + interest due). Below 1.0 means the business can't cover its debt service from operations. Lenders typically look for a comfortable buffer above 1.0. A common benchmark for term loans is around 1.25 or higher, depending on sector and policy. [Verify against internal policy.]
- **Leverage:** Debt/EBITDA, or Total Outside Liabilities / Tangible Net Worth (TOL/TNW), a common Indian banking measure.
- **Interest Coverage Ratio** = EBIT (or EBITDA) ÷ interest expense.
- **Liquidity:** current ratio, quick ratio, working-capital cycle (debtor, inventory and creditor days).
- **Profitability and trend:** margins and their direction over three to five years. Trend matters more than one year's number.

**Retail:**
- **FOIR (Fixed Obligation to Income Ratio):** total EMIs (including the new loan) ÷ net monthly income. Lenders set FOIR caps by income band and product.
- **LTV (Loan to Value):** loan amount ÷ collateral value, critical for home and gold loans. Regulators set LTV ceilings for some products. [Verify current RBI LTV caps for housing and gold loans.]
- **Bureau score:** for example CIBIL scores, which range from 300 to 900. Higher is better.

**Watch for:** related-party transactions, circular trading, rising receivables with flat sales, frequent auditor changes, cash-flow statements that don't reconcile with the P&L.

## 4. Covenants

Covenants are early warning tripwires written into the loan agreement.
- **Financial covenants:** for example maintain DSCR above 1.2x, Debt/EBITDA below 3.5x, minimum net worth.
- **Information covenants:** quarterly financials, stock statements, audited accounts within a set number of days.
- **Negative covenants:** no further borrowing, asset sales, dividend payments or change of ownership without lender consent.

A covenant breach doesn't automatically mean default. It's a trigger to **talk, reassess, re-rate and, if needed, restructure or tighten terms**.

## 5. Credit policy

A credit policy is the board-approved rulebook for lending. Typical contents:
- Target markets, products and segments, plus prohibited or restricted sectors.
- Eligibility criteria and minimum standards (ratings, ratios, bureau scores).
- Delegation of financial powers: who can approve what.
- Collateral and valuation standards, including haircuts and revaluation frequency.
- Pricing principles.
- Exposure and concentration limits.
- Exception and deviation handling: who can approve deviations, and how they're tracked.
- Monitoring, review frequency, watchlist and early warning procedures.
- Restructuring, write-off and recovery approach.

**Deviations** are a key health metric. A rising deviation rate, or deviations concentrated in one branch or relationship manager, often comes before an asset-quality problem.

## 6. Risk appetite

The **Risk Appetite Statement (RAS)** sets out how much risk the board is willing to take to meet its strategy. It's expressed through measurable metrics with thresholds.

Example credit risk appetite metrics:
- Gross NPA ratio ceiling
- Credit cost (provisions ÷ average loans) ceiling
- Maximum share of portfolio in rating grades below a set level
- Sector concentration limits, for example no more than X% of the book in commercial real estate
- Single-name and group exposure limits
- Unsecured lending as a share of the total book

Each metric typically carries an **early warning (amber) level** and a **limit (red) level**, plus a defined escalation path.

## 7. Concentration and exposure limits

Concentration risk is the risk that one borrower, group, sector or region causes outsized losses.

- **Large exposures (India):** RBI's Large Exposures Framework limits a bank's exposure to a single counterparty to 20% of its eligible capital base (Tier 1), extendable to 25% in exceptional cases with board approval, and to a group of connected counterparties to 25% of Tier 1. [Verify against the current RBI Large Exposures Framework and NBFC-specific limits.]
- **Sector limits:** set internally, based on the risk appetite and industry outlook.
- **Measuring concentration:** top-20 borrower share of the book, the Herfindahl–Hirschman Index (HHI) across sectors, geographic distribution.

## 8. Limit breaches and escalation

When a limit is breached:
1. Detect it promptly through automated monitoring, not month-end surprises.
2. Escalate according to policy, usually to the CRO and then the risk committee.
3. Analyse why: one big deal, a market move, a rating downgrade?
4. Decide: temporary approval with a remediation plan, risk transfer (sell-down, syndication, guarantees), or stop new business in that bucket.
5. Record and report the breach to the board risk committee.

## 9. Saying no constructively

A good credit risk manager is a **partner to the business, not a roadblock**. Instead of a flat "no", offer a structure:
- Reduce the amount or tenor.
- Ask for more collateral, a guarantee or a promoter contribution.
- Tighten covenants or the cash-flow escrow.
- Price for the risk.
- Approve in tranches linked to milestones.

The aim is to say: "Here's how this could become bankable."

## 10. Practice questions

1. A borrower has EBITDA of Rs 12 crore, taxes of Rs 2 crore, and annual principal plus interest due of Rs 9 crore. What's the approximate DSCR, and is it comfortable? *(Using EBITDA minus tax ≈ Rs 10 crore ÷ 9 ≈ 1.11x. Positive, but thin. Most policies want a bigger buffer.)*
2. A relationship manager says, "The collateral covers 200% of the loan, so the risk is low." How do you respond? *(Collateral lowers LGD, not PD. Check cash-flow capacity, and whether the collateral can actually be enforced and sold at that value.)*
3. Your commercial real estate book is at 95% of its sector limit, and a large new proposal would take it to 110%. What are your options? *(Escalate. Consider a sell-down or participation, restructure the deal size, or seek a temporary, time-bound limit increase with board approval and a plan to get back under the limit.)*
