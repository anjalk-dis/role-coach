# Client Needs Discovery and Relationship Building

*Knowledge area: Client discovery and relationship building. Audience: relationship managers in wealth, priority and business banking. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. Why discovery comes before products

New relationship managers often open with a product: a fund, an insurance plan, a loan offer. Clients can sense this and respond with a polite "I will think about it". Discovery reverses the order. You first understand the person and their money, then recommend. A client who feels understood gives you more information, more of their wallet and more referrals.

Discovery is not an interrogation. It is a conversation in which the client does most of the talking. A useful rule of thumb is that in a first meeting the client should speak for well over half the time.

## 2. The consultative questioning ladder

Move from broad to specific.

- **Situation questions:** "Tell me about your work and your family." "Where are your savings today?"
- **Goal questions:** "What are you hoping your money will do for you in the next five and fifteen years?" "What does a good retirement look like for you?"
- **Problem questions:** "What worries you most about your finances?" "Has anything disappointed you with past investments or banks?"
- **Implication questions:** "If the school fees for two children rise as they have, how would that affect your plans?"
- **Need-payoff questions:** "If we set aside a separate pool for that goal, would that give you more peace of mind?"

Prefer open questions (what, how, tell me about) over closed ones. Listen for what the client does not say: a business owner who never mentions succession, or a salaried client who never mentions life cover.

## 3. What to capture in the first meeting

- Personal: age, family, dependents, parents' needs, plans for children's education and marriage.
- Income and cash flow: salary or business income, bonuses, regular outflows, EMIs.
- Assets and liabilities: bank balances, fixed deposits, mutual funds, shares, property, gold, insurance, loans.
- Goals with amounts and dates, for example "Rs 1.5 crore for the child's higher education in 12 years".
- Risk attitude and past experience, including bad experiences.
- Relationships with other banks, advisers and the family's financial decision maker.
- KYC and compliance information required by the bank. [Verify: current KYC and risk categorisation requirements.]

Record it in the bank's CRM straight after the meeting, while it is fresh.

## 4. Segmenting your book

You cannot serve every client equally. Segment the portfolio so effort follows value and potential.

| Segment | Typical profile | Service rhythm |
|---|---|---|
| Top clients | High balances or high potential, multiple products | Personal contact monthly, review quarterly |
| Core clients | Steady balances, a few products | Contact every one to two months, review half-yearly |
| Emerging | Young professionals, small business owners with growth potential | Regular touchpoints, educate, build the base |
| Dormant or at risk | Falling balances, no contact | Re-engagement plan |

Segment by **current relationship value** and **potential**, not only by today's balance. A founder with Rs 8 lakh in the account but a business turning over Rs 20 crore is a high-potential client. [Verify: your bank's own segment thresholds.]

## 5. Share of wallet and account planning

**Share of wallet** is the proportion of a client's total financial business that you hold. If a client invests Rs 60 lakh in total and Rs 15 lakh is with you, your share is 25 percent. Growing it responsibly means helping the client consolidate where it truly serves them, not simply capturing balances.

An **account plan** for a key client contains:

1. Snapshot of the relationship: products held, balances, revenue.
2. Client goals and life events in the next 12 to 24 months.
3. Gaps: protection, retirement, liquidity, business credit, family needs.
4. Opportunities ranked by client benefit first, bank benefit second.
5. Next three actions with dates.
6. Risks: competitor approach, unhappy service history, rate sensitivity.

Review each plan at least quarterly.

## 6. Worked example: a business owner with idle money

Mr Arvind Kulkarni, 46, runs an auto components unit with a turnover of about Rs 18 crore. He banks with you for a current account and a working capital limit of Rs 1.5 crore. His personal savings of about Rs 45 lakh sit in a savings account, and he has a term life cover of Rs 50 lakh only.

A product-first RM would offer a fund. A consultative RM asks: "How do you keep personal and business money separate? What would happen to the family and the unit if something happened to you?" Arvind reveals that a new machine purchase of Rs 60 lakh is planned in 18 months, that his daughter starts college in 4 years, and that his brother is his partner.

The account plan then has priorities:

- Protection: review life and key-person cover relative to income and business loans. [Verify: current underwriting norms with your insurance partner.]
- Liquidity: keep about 6 months of personal expenses in a liquid or sweep option.
- Goal buckets: a short-term bucket for the machine, a medium-term bucket for the education goal.
- Business: a term loan discussion for the machine, which supports the corporate relationship.

Note that the first three actions benefit the client even before any loan sale.

## 7. Building trust and retention

Trust is built through repeated small proofs.

- **Do what you say.** If you promise a call on Thursday, call on Thursday.
- **Be honest about risk and cost.** Clients forgive a fall in markets far more readily than a surprise.
- **Be proactive.** Contact clients when rates change, a goal date approaches or markets fall sharply, not only when you have a target.
- **Mark life events.** Birthdays, a child's admission, a new business, retirement.
- **Be reachable.** Respond quickly or tell the client when you will.
- **Handle your own transfers well.** RMs change; a clean handover keeps the client.

Early signs of attrition: balances shifting out, fewer calls answered, questions about a competitor's offer, a new adviser appearing in conversation. Respond with a conversation, not a discount.

## 8. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Talking about products in the first ten minutes.
- Treating the first meeting as a form to fill rather than a conversation.
- Chasing the largest balance and neglecting emerging clients with potential.
- Confusing share of wallet with pressure to move money.
- Recording nothing, so the next RM or colleague starts from zero.
- Calling only at month end when targets are due.
- Coaching point: after every meeting, write down the client's goal in their own words and one action you owe them.

## 9. Practice questions

**1. A new client says, "I just want a good fixed deposit rate." What do you do?**
*(Answer the question honestly, then ask permission to understand the goal behind it: what the money is for and when it is needed. The FD may be right, but you may uncover liquidity, tax or protection needs.)*

**2. How would you estimate your share of wallet with a client?**
*(Ask about total investments and banking across institutions, compare it with what you hold, and treat the gap as an opportunity to serve better, not a number to extract.)*

**3. A top client has stopped taking your calls and balances are falling. What next?**
*(Review recent service history, reach out through a different channel with a useful reason such as a portfolio review, listen without defending, and involve your manager if there is a service failure to fix.)*
