# Financial Planning and Product Suitability

*Knowledge area: Financial planning and product suitability. Audience: relationship managers in wealth, priority and business banking. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation against current SEBI, AMFI, IRDAI and RBI rules.*

## 1. The planning logic

Good recommendations follow a sequence: **goals, then risk profile, then asset allocation, then products**. Products come last. An RM who starts with the product list is selling; one who starts with goals is advising.

A basic planning order for most households:

1. **Protection:** term life insurance and adequate health cover, so that one event does not wipe out the plan.
2. **Liquidity:** an emergency reserve, commonly 3 to 6 months of expenses, in savings, sweep, liquid fund or short deposit.
3. **Goal investing:** assets matched to each goal's time horizon.
4. **Debt management:** prepaying high-cost loans where sensible.
5. **Estate and succession:** nominations, wills, family arrangements.

## 2. Risk profiling

A risk profile has three parts.

- **Risk capacity:** how much loss the client can absorb financially. Depends on income stability, dependents, existing assets and liabilities.
- **Risk tolerance (attitude):** how the client feels about volatility. Gauged through a questionnaire and conversation about past behaviour, for example "What did you do in March 2020?"
- **Time horizon:** when the money is needed.

The **lowest** of capacity and tolerance should usually guide the profile. A 35-year-old founder may have a high tolerance but low capacity if all wealth is locked in the business. Document the profile, use the bank's approved tool, and revisit it periodically or at life events. ## 3. Asset allocation by horizon

Asset allocation drives most of the long-run outcome. A common way to explain it:

| Horizon | Typical focus |
|---|---|
| Under 1 year | Savings, sweep, liquid and money market options, short deposits |
| 1 to 3 years | Short-duration debt, deposits, conservative hybrid options |
| 3 to 5 years | Balanced mix of debt and equity |
| 5 years and above | Higher equity share, with debt for stability |

These are illustrations, not rules. The right mix depends on the client. Explain that equity is volatile in the short term and that returns are not guaranteed.

## 4. Product basics an RM must explain clearly

**Deposits.** Fixed and recurring deposits give predictable interest, taxable at the client's slab rate. Deposit insurance cover applies per depositor per bank up to a limit. [Verify: current DICGC limit, currently understood as Rs 5 lakh per depositor per bank.]

**Mutual funds.** Pooled investments managed by an AMC. Categories include liquid, debt, hybrid, equity, index and others as defined by SEBI. Explain the risk-o-meter, expense ratio, exit load and that past performance does not guarantee future returns. Direct plans have lower expenses than regular plans, and the RM's bank may earn distribution commission on regular plans, which must be disclosed. [Verify: current commission disclosure requirements.] SIPs help investors build discipline and average the purchase cost.

**Insurance.** Term plans provide pure protection at low cost. Endowment, ULIP and similar plans combine insurance and investment, with higher costs and longer lock-ins. Health insurance should be sized to medical costs in the client's city. Always disclose lock-in, charges, surrender terms and free-look period. [Verify: current IRDAI free-look and disclosure norms.]

**Bonds and structured products.** Understand the issuer, credit rating, liquidity and tax. Many are complex and suited only to informed, higher-risk clients. [Verify: current rules on distribution of complex products to retail clients.]

**Loans.** Home loans, loans against securities, business term loans and working capital. Explain the interest rate type, processing fees, prepayment terms and total cost. Under RBI guidance, banks should provide a key fact statement. [Verify: current RBI requirements on key fact statement and floating rate loan reset and charges.]

## 5. Suitability and mis-selling rules

**Suitability** means the product matches the client's needs, risk profile, horizon and understanding. It is the central legal and ethical test. Key principles that apply across the SEBI, AMFI, IRDAI and RBI frameworks:

- Know your client and understand their situation before recommending.
- Recommend only products suitable for that client's profile, and document the reason.
- Disclose risks, costs, commissions and conflicts of interest clearly and in plain language.
- Do not promise or imply guaranteed returns on market-linked products.
- Do not mislead about the nature of a product, for example presenting an insurance plan as a fixed deposit.
- Do not pressure clients or sell products tied to loan sanction without consent. Bundled or tied sales are a recognised mis-selling risk.
- Keep records of advice, disclosures and consent.
- Distributors of mutual funds should be AMFI-registered (ARN) and hold valid certification. [Verify: current certification and registration requirements for the RM role.]

A useful self-check before each recommendation: "Would I be comfortable explaining this recommendation, my reasons and my commission to the client's family and to my compliance officer?"

## 6. Worked example: a retired couple and a high-yield plan

Mr and Mrs Nair, 63 and 60, retired from government service. They have Rs 1.2 crore from retirement benefits, a pension of Rs 55,000 a month, and a son in Dubai who may send money occasionally. They ask for something that gives "better than FD returns, a little higher, and no tension".

An unsuitable response: put the full Rs 1.2 crore in a long-lock-in endowment with a higher commission because it shows a "maturity benefit".

A suitable process:

1. Establish needs: monthly expenses about Rs 50,000, medical risk, no big goals. The pension covers most expenses.
2. Set aside a medical reserve of Rs 10 lakh in liquid or deposit form, and review the health cover.
3. Keep about 3 years of any shortfall in deposits or short-duration debt.
4. Place the balance in a diversified mix of deposits, high-quality debt and a modest equity-oriented hybrid allocation, such as 20 percent, so that inflation does not erode their income over a 25-year horizon. [Verify: appropriate hybrid category for senior clients per bank policy.]
5. Explain that the debt and hybrid portions can fall in value and that returns are not guaranteed. Provide the risk-o-meter and the cost disclosure in writing.
6. Nominate on all products and discuss a will.

The approach gives the couple stability first, growth second, and a clear record of why each product was chosen.

## 7. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Recommending the product with the best incentive rather than the best fit.
- Using one risk profile forever, even after a client's circumstances change.
- Saying "guaranteed" or "safe" for market-linked products.
- Selling long lock-in products to senior citizens or to clients who may need liquidity.
- Ignoring existing holdings and duplicating products the client already owns.
- Failing to explain charges, exit loads and tax in simple language.
- Coaching point: always state three things in a recommendation: why it suits the goal, what can go wrong, and what it costs.

## 8. Practice questions

**1. A client in his late twenties wants to invest in equity funds for a house purchase in 2 years. What do you say?**
*(A 2-year horizon is short for equity because of volatility. Discuss the goal date, suggest lower-risk options such as short-duration debt or deposits for the down payment, and document the discussion.)*

**2. A client asks you to guarantee the return of a mutual fund. How do you respond?**
*(You cannot guarantee returns on market-linked products. Explain risk and past performance disclaimers, show historical ranges fairly, and suggest a deposit if certainty is the priority.)*

**3. Why is a term plan usually recommended before an investment-linked insurance plan?**
*(Term cover gives the largest protection per rupee of premium, keeps protection and investment separate, and avoids the higher charges and lock-ins of combined products, so the household is covered first.)*
