# Sales Conversations, Objections and Ethics

*Knowledge area: Sales conversations, objection handling, complaints and ethical practice. Audience: relationship managers in wealth, priority and business banking. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. Sales as service

In a relationship role, a good sale is one that the client would still be glad about three years later. That view keeps conversations honest and, in practice, produces better retention and referrals than pressure does. This document covers how to run a review meeting, handle objections, cross-sell ethically, deal with complaints and manage your book.

## 2. Structuring a review meeting

A review meeting is the most important routine in the RM's calendar. Use a simple structure.

1. **Prepare.** Read the portfolio, recent transactions, service history and the last meeting's notes. Check performance against benchmark and goals. Identify one or two topics to raise.
2. **Open warmly.** Thank the client, set the agenda and time, and ask what they want from the meeting.
3. **Review the goals first.** "Last time you wanted Rs 40 lakh for the house down payment by 2028. Let us see where we stand."
4. **Present performance honestly.** Show gains and losses, explain drivers in plain language, and compare against the right benchmark and goal, not against a friend's tip.
5. **Discuss changes.** Life events, risk appetite, rebalancing, new needs.
6. **Recommend, with reasons.** Offer one to three options, with risks, costs and alternatives.
7. **Agree actions.** Who does what, by when.
8. **Follow up in writing** within a day, and diarise the next review.

## 3. Handling objections

An objection is information. It tells you what the client is worried about. The basic method is **listen, acknowledge, clarify, respond, check**.

| Objection | What may be behind it | A helpful response |
|---|---|---|
| "The fees are too high." | Unclear value, or comparison with direct or online options | Explain exactly what is charged and what is included, show direct versus regular options honestly, and let the client choose. |
| "Markets have fallen. I want out." | Fear and loss aversion | Revisit the goal and horizon, review the allocation, show the long-term picture, and respect the decision if the risk profile has genuinely changed. |
| "I need to think about it." | Not enough clarity or trust, or timing | Ask what specifically they want to think over and offer a summary in writing, without pressure. |
| "My friend got a better return elsewhere." | Comparison without context | Ask what the friend invested in and the risk taken, and compare like with like. |
| "I already have a bank adviser for that." | Loyalty or inertia | Ask what is working and what is missing, and offer a second opinion on one area. |

Never respond to an objection with a misleading claim, an unsupported promise or time pressure ("the offer ends today"). Where a client says no, accept it and note the date to revisit.

## 4. Cross-selling without pushing

Cross-selling is acceptable when it solves a real need. Use these guidelines.

- **Start from a gap.** "You mentioned your daughter's college in four years. Shall we set up a monthly plan for it?" is better than "Do you want a mutual fund?"
- **Offer one thing at a time.** A client given six products usually takes none.
- **Link to an event.** Home loan sanction, a bonus, a business expansion, retirement.
- **Be transparent about the benefit to the bank** where relevant.
- **Never tie** a sale to an unrelated service, for example hinting that a loan depends on buying insurance. Tied or bundled selling without informed consent is a recognised mis-selling practice under regulatory guidance. [Verify: current RBI and IRDAI guidance on tied sales and bancassurance conduct.]
- **Respect no.** A refusal is not a failure; pushing after a clear no damages trust and may breach conduct rules.

## 5. Worked example: a hesitant client after a market fall

Ms Priya Menon, 41, a senior manager, started a Rs 25,000 monthly SIP in equity funds 18 months ago. Her portfolio of about Rs 6 lakh is now down 9 percent after a market fall, and she calls to say she wants to stop the SIP and move to a fixed deposit.

A pressure-led RM says: "Do not worry, markets always come back." A better conversation goes:

1. **Listen:** "Tell me what is worrying you most." Priya says she saw a news headline and her husband's company is cutting jobs.
2. **Acknowledge:** "That is a real concern, and it makes sense to feel cautious."
3. **Clarify:** The goal is her son's education in 10 years, plus an emergency need is possible because of the job risk at home.
4. **Respond with options:** Build 6 months of expenses, around Rs 3.5 lakh, in a liquid reserve first. Continue a smaller SIP of Rs 15,000 into the equity funds for the 10-year goal. Review the risk profile and adjust the allocation if her tolerance has genuinely changed.
5. **Check:** "Does that feel comfortable?" Write down the agreed plan and the risks.

Priya keeps investing at a level she can sustain, and the relationship strengthens because the RM solved the real problem, which was liquidity and security, not only volatility.

## 6. Handling complaints

A well-handled complaint can strengthen loyalty. A poorly handled one often ends the relationship and may escalate to the bank's grievance cell, the RBI Ombudsman or SEBI SCORES. [Verify: current RBI Integrated Ombudsman Scheme and bank turnaround timelines.]

Steps:

1. **Listen fully** without interrupting or defending.
2. **Apologise for the experience** and take ownership: "I am sorry this happened. I will own getting this resolved."
3. **Log the complaint** in the bank's system as required, with a reference number.
4. **Investigate and involve** the right teams or your manager.
5. **Communicate** the status and timeline, and keep your word on follow-ups.
6. **Resolve fairly.** Where the bank was at fault, remedy it, including refunds where appropriate.
7. **Learn.** Note the root cause and tell your manager if it is a pattern.

Do not discourage a client from escalating, and do not argue about who was at fault in the heat of the moment. RBI's fair practices expectations emphasise transparency, timely redress and no coercion.

## 7. Ethics and compliance in daily practice

- Do your KYC and due diligence properly, and escalate suspicious transactions as per the bank's AML process. Never tip off the client about a suspicious transaction report. [Verify: current PMLA and RBI KYC requirements.]
- Keep client information confidential and use only authorised channels. Avoid personal WhatsApp for advice or order instructions unless bank policy permits. [Verify: bank policy.]
- Declare and manage conflicts of interest, such as incentives or relatives' accounts.
- Do not take orders or credentials from clients informally, share passwords or OTPs, or operate accounts on behalf of clients.
- Do not accept gifts beyond policy limits.
- Never create false records, split transactions to avoid thresholds, or help a client do so.
- When unsure, ask compliance before you act, not afterwards.

## 8. Managing your book and pipeline

- **Weekly rhythm:** a set time for calls to top clients, a set time for new prospecting, and a set time for follow-ups and admin.
- **Pipeline stages:** lead, first meeting, discovery done, proposal, decision, onboarding, review. Track each opportunity with the next step and date.
- **Prioritise** by client value, potential and urgency, such as a maturing deposit or an upcoming life event.
- **Watch attrition signals:** falling balances, unanswered calls, complaints.
- **Targets vs. interest:** if a target pressure conflicts with client interest, raise it with your manager. A mis-sold product costs far more than a missed target.
- **Capacity:** a typical RM can only maintain meaningful relationships with a limited number of clients, so use segmentation to decide where your time goes.

## 9. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Starting a pitch before understanding the objection.
- Offering discounts instead of solving the real concern.
- Cross-selling an unrelated product at the first conversation.
- Avoiding bad news. Call early when markets fall or a mistake occurs.
- Treating complaints as personal attacks.
- Using month-end pressure to push products.
- Coaching point: end each client conversation by asking, "What else is on your mind?" The real concern often appears in the last minute.

## 10. Practice questions

**1. A client says, "Your fund charges are too high compared with online platforms." How do you respond?**
*(Acknowledge, explain what the charges cover and show direct versus regular plan differences honestly. Let the client decide, and do not become defensive or hide the commission.)*

**2. Your target is due at month end and a client who needs liquidity could buy a long lock-in plan that pays a good incentive. What do you do?**
*(Do not sell it. Suitability and client interest come first; escalate target pressure to your manager and keep the record of why the plan was not recommended.)*

**3. A client complains that a fund switch you suggested lost money and says you mis-sold it. What are your first steps?**
*(Listen without defending, log the complaint, review the file, including the risk profile and disclosures, involve your manager and compliance, and communicate a timeline.)*
