# Term Insurance: Product Knowledge and Selling

*Knowledge area: Term insurance. Audience: insurance sales professionals in India. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. What term insurance is

Term insurance is **pure protection**. You pay a premium for a fixed period. If the life insured dies during that period, the nominee receives the **sum assured**. If the life insured survives the term, the policy ends and nothing is paid. The one exception is **return of premium (TROP)**, covered in section 2.

With no savings component, nearly all of the premium buys risk cover, so term is the **cheapest cover per rupee**. A healthy 30-year-old non-smoker can often get ₹1 crore for roughly ₹800 to ₹1,000 a month. [Verify.]

Contrast with other products:

| Product | Main purpose | Death benefit | Maturity benefit | Cover per rupee |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Term plan | Protection | Sum assured | None (except TROP) | Highest |
| Endowment / traditional savings plan | Savings plus small cover | Sum assured plus bonuses | Yes | Low |
| ULIP | Market-linked investment plus cover | Higher of sum assured or fund value (varies) | Fund value | Low |

## 2. Variants

- **Level cover:** the sum assured stays constant. The most common and simplest.
- **Increasing cover:** the sum assured rises yearly, for example 5 percent, to offset inflation. The premium is higher. [Verify.]
- **Decreasing cover (loan protection):** the sum assured falls with a reducing loan balance. Cheaper, but fits only a loan-protection need.
- **Return of premium (TROP):** premiums paid (usually excluding GST and rider premiums [Verify]) come back if the policyholder survives the term. The premium is much higher. Offer it only to someone who clearly wants it, never as an investment.
- **Whole-life term (to age 99 or 100):** priced higher, suits legacy needs.
- **Limited pay / single pay:** premiums are paid for a shorter period or once, while cover runs the full term.
- **Joint life:** two lives, usually spouses, in one policy. [Verify: first-death only or both.]
- **Special exit / zero-cost exit options:** an exit at a set age with a defined payout under stated conditions. [Verify: structure by insurer.]
- **Saral Jeevan Bima:** the IRDAI standard term product with common terms across insurers. [Verify: sum-assured range, entry ages and term limits.]

## 3. Riders

Riders are add-ons with their own premium and terms.

- **Accidental death benefit (ADB):** an extra sum if death is accidental.
- **Critical illness (CI):** a lump sum on diagnosis of a listed illness such as cancer or heart attack, usually with a waiting and survival period. [Verify.]
- **Waiver of premium:** future premiums are waived on defined disability or critical illness, and cover continues.
- **Terminal illness benefit:** an advance payout on terminal diagnosis, often built in at no extra cost. [Verify.]
- **Disability:** a lump sum or monthly income on defined total and permanent disability.

Rider caps exist, often on total rider premium as a share of base premium. [Verify: current IRDAI rules and insurer caps.] Match riders to the need rather than stacking all of them.

## 4. How much cover

**Human Life Value (HLV)** is the present value of the future income a person will earn to support the family.

**Rule of thumb:** 10 to 15 times annual income. Quick, but it ignores liabilities and existing assets.

**Needs-based method:**

Cover needed = liabilities + future goals + years of household expenses − existing assets and existing cover.

**Worked example.** Rahul is 32, earns ₹12 lakh a year, has a home loan of ₹40 lakh outstanding, and has two children aged 3 and 1.

- Liabilities: home loan ₹40 lakh.
- Future goals: education ₹25 lakh per child, so ₹50 lakh. Other goals are left out.
- Household expenses: ₹4.8 lakh a year for 12 years, about ₹58 lakh. Simplified, without inflation.
- Total need: ₹40 lakh + ₹50 lakh + ₹58 lakh = ₹148 lakh.
- Less existing assets and cover: say ₹20 lakh in savings and an employer group cover of ₹10 lakh. That is ₹30 lakh.
- Net need: ₹118 lakh, so about **₹1.2 crore**.

The income multiple gives ₹1.2 to ₹1.8 crore, so the methods broadly agree. 

## 5. Policy term and premium drivers

Premium depends on:

- **Age at entry:** each year of delay raises the premium. Buying early locks in a lower rate.
- **Smoker status:** smokers often pay 50 to 100 percent more. [Verify.]
- **Gender:** women often pay less.
- **Health:** BMI, history, test results.
- **Sum assured and policy term:** a bigger cover and a longer term cost more.
- **Riders** and **payment frequency:** annual payment is usually cheaper than monthly.

**Choosing the term:** run cover until earning life ends or the children are independent and loans cleared, often age 60 or 65.

**Illustrative premium table (illustrative only, varies by insurer):**

| Profile | Cover | Term | Approx. annual premium (before GST changes) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Male, 30, non-smoker | ₹1 crore | 30 years | ₹10,000 to ₹12,000 |
| Male, 30, smoker | ₹1 crore | 30 years | ₹16,000 to ₹20,000 |
| Female, 30, non-smoker | ₹1 crore | 30 years | ₹8,500 to ₹10,500 |

Always use the insurer's live quote.

**Per-day cost:** ₹12,000 a year is about ₹33 a day, less than a cup of chai and a samosa. Say it this way: "For about ₹33 a day, your family has ₹1 crore behind them."

## 6. Underwriting

- **Medical tests:** depending on age and cover, blood and urine tests, ECG and others.
- **Tele-medical:** a phone or video call about health history.
- **Financial underwriting / income proof:** salary slips, ITR or bank statements to confirm that the cover is in line with income.
- **Disclosure of health and habits:** smoking, alcohol, tobacco, existing conditions, family history, medications, past surgeries, hazardous hobbies and occupation.

**Honest disclosure protects the claim.** Under **Section 45 of the Insurance Act, 1938** (as amended), an insurer's ability to call a policy into question is time-limited. Within the first three years, a policy can be repudiated on grounds of fraud or material misstatement, and the insurer must follow a defined process. After three years, repudiation is allowed only on proven fraud. [Verify: exact conditions, notice requirements and burden of proof.] The practical message for the client: hiding a condition to save premium can cost the family the claim. Never tell a client to skip a question.

## 7. Claims

**How a claim works:**

The nominee informs the insurer and submits the claim form and documents. The insurer verifies, may investigate (especially early claims), then settles or rejects with written reasons.

**Typical documents:** claim form, policy document, death certificate, nominee ID and bank details, and for unnatural death an FIR and post-mortem report. [Verify.]

**Claim settlement ratio (CSR):**

- **By number:** the share of claims settled out of claims received.
- **By amount:** the share of the claimed rupee amount that was paid.

The two can differ. A high ratio by number with a lower one by amount can mean large claims were declined more often.

How to discuss CSR responsibly:

- Quote the year and source (insurer disclosure or IRDAI annual report), and show both by-number and by-amount. [Verify.]
- Never say "guaranteed to pay". A claim is paid when the policy is valid and terms are met.
- Explain that accurate disclosure and timely premiums protect the claim.

**Payout options:** a lump sum; monthly income over a set number of years; or a combination (for example, 50 percent lump sum and the rest as monthly income). 

## 8. Nomination, MWPA and tax

**Nomination:** the policyholder names a nominee who receives the money. Keep it updated after marriage or the birth of a child.

**MWPA policies:** for a married man, a policy taken under the **Married Women's Property Act, 1874** is held in trust for his wife and children. The proceeds are generally protected from his creditors and cannot be claimed by his other relatives. It suits business owners and anyone with debt risk, and must be opted for at issue. [Verify: process, eligibility and legal effect.]

**Tax:**

- **Section 80C:** premiums qualify for a deduction within the overall ₹1.5 lakh limit, but only under the **old tax regime**. [Verify.]
- **Section 10(10D):** the death benefit paid to the nominee is generally tax-free. [Verify: conditions and any limits that apply to other types of policies.]
- **GST:** individual life insurance premiums, including term, have been **exempt from GST since 22 September 2025**. Earlier it was 18 percent. The premium drop may be **less than 18 percent**, because insurers can no longer claim input tax credit and may have adjusted base prices. Do not promise "18 percent cheaper". Quote the live premium. [Verify: current treatment of riders and group policies.]

## 9. Term insurance for homemakers

The key idea: a homemaker's work has real economic value, because the family would otherwise pay for it.

### Why it matters

If a homemaker dies, the family faces new costs at once, and the husband may need to cut his work hours. That is a real financial loss, even though no salary stops.

**Replacement-cost approach, worked example.** Priya is 34. Her youngest child is 5. Estimate what it costs to replace her contribution:

- Cook and housekeeping help, childcare, home tuition support, eldercare help and household management: about **₹35,000 a month**.
- That is ₹4.2 lakh a year.
- Until the youngest child turns 18: 13 years, so ₹4.2 lakh × 13 = **₹54.6 lakh**.
- Add a buffer for inflation, the husband's reduced work hours and counselling or transition costs: say ₹10 to ₹15 lakh.
- Recommended cover: roughly **₹65 to ₹70 lakh**, so a conversation about **₹50 lakh to ₹1 crore** is reasonable.

### Eligibility reality

Be open about this early.

- Insurers usually look at the **husband's income and existing cover** because the homemaker has no income.
- Common practice caps the homemaker's cover at about **50 to 100 percent of the husband's cover**, and often at ₹50 lakh to ₹1 crore. [Verify.]
- The husband's income may need to cross a threshold, often around ₹5 lakh a year or more, and her **education level** can affect limits. [Verify.]
- All of this varies by insurer. Check the insurer's current rules before quoting a figure.

### The conversation

- **Speak to both spouses together.** The decision is a household decision, and often the husband is the one who has to agree.
- **Use respectful words.** Never say "she doesn't earn". Say "she runs the household".
- **Ask discovery questions:**
  - "Who handles the children's mornings, school pickup and homework?"
  - "If Priya were not around for a month, what would you need to arrange, and what would it cost?"
  - "Do you have parents at home who depend on her care?"
- **Quantify the contribution** with the ₹35,000-a-month exercise, and let them correct the numbers.
- **Make it affordable:** a ₹50 lakh cover for a healthy woman in her thirties may cost only a few thousand rupees a year, so under ₹10 to ₹15 a day. [Verify against live quote.]
- **Separate plan vs joint plan:** a separate plan gives each spouse independent cover, and a claim on one does not end the other's. A joint plan can be simpler to manage but may pay out once and then end. [Verify.]

### Objection handling

**"She doesn't earn, why insure her?"**
*"You're right that there's no salary. But if she were not there, you'd be paying for childcare, cooking, tuition help and more. That's about ₹35,000 a month. The cover is for that cost, not for her salary."*

**"My husband's cover is enough."**
*"His cover protects the family if he is gone. It does not pay for the running of the home if she is gone. Those are two different risks."*

**"A joint plan is simpler."**
*"It can be. The trade-off is that after one claim the policy may end, leaving the other partner uncovered. Let me show you both side by side."* [Verify.]

**"The money is wasted if nothing happens."**
*"You are paying for peace of mind, like home or health insurance. If you want something back at the end, there is a return-of-premium option, but it costs more, and I would not treat it as an investment. Shall I show you both prices?"*

**"We need to ask the family / think about it."**
*"Of course. Would it help if I send a one-page summary with the numbers so you can discuss it? What would you like to be clear on before we speak again?"*

### Compliance guardrails

- Disclose eligibility caps and the dependence on the husband's income and cover. Do not hide them.
- Make no misleading claims about returns, claims or tax. Check suitability.
- Do not sell TROP as an investment or savings product.
- Both spouses must fill the proposal form honestly, including her health and habits.

### Sample 60-second pitch

*"Priya ji, Rahul ji, can I ask something first? Who looks after the children's mornings, the cooking, homework and your parents' needs? ... That is a full-time job, and a very important one. Now imagine, God forbid, that Priya was not there. You would need help for the home and the children, and Rahul ji may need to take time off work. We estimated about ₹35,000 a month. Over 13 years that is about ₹55 lakh. A cover of ₹50 lakh to ₹1 crore for Priya would give the family that support. For a healthy woman her age it may cost only a few rupees a day, and I will confirm the exact figure and the insurer's limits with you today. It is not about her salary. It is about protecting the home she runs. Would you like me to show you a plan for Priya alongside Rahul's?"*

## 10. Other key segments

- **Young earners (22 to 30):** low premiums, easy underwriting, rates locked in early.
- **New parents:** sudden dependants. Revisit cover size and nominee.
- **Home-loan borrowers:** cover at least the loan plus family expenses. Level cover is often better for the family than decreasing cover.
- **Self-employed and business owners:** irregular income makes financial underwriting harder. Ask for ITR and bank statements. Consider MWPA.
- **NRIs:** eligibility, medicals, premium routes and claim documents differ. [Verify.]

## 11. Common mistakes / coaching points

- Selling a savings plan when the client needs protection.
- Quoting a sum assured without a needs calculation.
- Skipping exclusions, waiting periods and rider limits.
- Over-selling claim settlement ratio, or quoting it without context.
- Ignoring the homemaker as a cover need.
- Pushing TROP as an investment.
- Promising "18 percent cheaper" after the GST change.
- Letting the client hide health or habit details to get a lower price.

## 12. Practice questions

1. A homemaker's family says, "She doesn't earn, so why spend on her cover?" How do you answer?
   *(Answer: Acknowledge, then explain the replacement cost of her work, about ₹35,000 a month, and disclose that her cover is linked to her husband's income and cover.)*

2. A client asks, "Is a 98 percent claim settlement ratio a guarantee that my claim will be paid?" What do you say?
   *(Answer: No. It shows past experience, not a promise. Explain by-number versus by-amount, and stress accurate disclosure.)*

3. A 32-year-old earns ₹12 lakh a year, has a ₹40 lakh home loan and two young children. How would you decide the cover?
   *(Answer: Use the needs-based method, which gives about ₹1.2 crore in section 4, and sense-check it against 10 to 15 times income.)*
