# Health Insurance Products and Selling

*Knowledge area: Health insurance products and selling. Audience: health insurance advisors selling retail cover to individuals and families. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation against current IRDAI regulations and insurer product wordings.*

## 1. Why health cover matters

A hospital bill is the single most common reason a middle-class Indian family dips into savings or borrows. Treatment costs rise faster than general inflation, and a single admission for a cardiac or orthopaedic procedure can run into several lakh. Health insurance converts an unpredictable, large bill into a predictable, small premium. The advisor's job is to match cover to the family's real exposure, not to push the highest sum insured or the lowest premium.

## 2. Indemnity versus fixed benefit

- **Indemnity (hospitalisation) plans** reimburse the actual eligible bill up to the sum insured. This is the core product for almost every family.
- **Fixed benefit plans** pay a pre-agreed amount on a defined event, such as a daily cash amount during hospitalisation or a lump sum on diagnosis of a listed condition. The payout does not depend on the bill.
- **Critical illness (CI) cover** is a fixed-benefit lump sum on diagnosis of a listed illness such as cancer, heart attack or stroke. It helps with income loss and non-medical costs. It does **not** replace a hospitalisation plan.

Coaching line: "Indemnity pays the hospital. Critical illness pays you. Most families need the first for sure, and the second only if the budget allows."

## 3. Individual versus family floater

- **Individual plan:** each member has their own sum insured. Premium is higher, but each person's cover is independent. Better when members have very different ages or health risks.
- **Family floater:** one sum insured shared by all members. Premium is lower, and any one member can use the entire amount. Best for young families with children.
- Premium on a floater is usually based on the age of the eldest member. Adding parents to a young couple's floater often makes it expensive and dilutes the cover. Parents are generally better placed on a **separate** policy.
- Floater risk: if two members claim in one year, the shared sum insured can run down quickly. Restoration (section 9) partly addresses this.

## 4. Choosing the sum insured

Three inputs drive the number: **city of treatment**, **family profile** and **medical inflation**.

- Metro hospitals (Mumbai, Delhi NCR, Bengaluru, Pune) cost materially more than tier-2 cities. Many families treat in a metro even if they live elsewhere.
- Medical inflation is often quoted at around 10-14% a year. [Verify: use the insurer's or an industry body's current figure.]
- A common starting benchmark for a metro family is a base cover of Rs 10 to 25 lakh, with a super top-up above it.

**Worked example.** The Kulkarnis, a family of four in Pune (two parents aged 36 and 34, two children aged 6 and 3), earn Rs 18 lakh a year combined. Their employer gives a group cover of Rs 5 lakh each.

- Cost of a major cardiac procedure with a five-day stay in a Pune private hospital today: about Rs 6 to 8 lakh.
- At roughly 12% inflation, the same procedure costs about Rs 12 to 14 lakh in six years.
- Recommended structure: a Rs 10 lakh family floater as the base, plus a Rs 40 lakh super top-up with a Rs 10 lakh deductible. Total cover is Rs 50 lakh against a catastrophic bill.
- Indicative annual premium: about Rs 18,000 to 22,000 for the floater and Rs 5,000 to 7,000 for the super top-up. [Verify: premiums vary by insurer, age and city; use a live quote.]
- Compare: a Rs 50 lakh single floater for the same family could cost over Rs 35,000. Layering gives higher cover at a lower price.

Coaching point: always show the customer a **real bill range**, not a formula.

## 5. Waiting periods

Waiting periods are periods during which certain claims are not payable. Explain them as "the insurer's cooling-off before it covers known or planned conditions."

- **Initial waiting period:** typically 30 days from policy start for illness claims. Accidents are normally covered from day one. [Verify: insurer wording.]
- **Pre-existing disease (PED) waiting period:** conditions the customer had before buying. IRDAI's current framework limits this to a maximum of three years. [Verify: current maximum under the latest IRDAI regulations and master circular; it was earlier up to four years.]
- **Specific illness waiting period:** named conditions such as cataract, hernia, joint replacement or kidney stones are excluded for an initial period, commonly one to three years. [Verify: current maximum.]
- **Moratorium:** after a continuous coverage period, an insurer cannot reject a claim for non-disclosure, except for proven fraud. IRDAI's current framework sets this at five years. [Verify: current period.]
- Waiting periods can often be **reduced or waived** for an additional premium on some plans. Always confirm in the policy wording.

Honest disclosure of pre-existing conditions is essential. An undisclosed diabetes or hypertension history is the commonest ground for claim dispute.

## 6. Room rent limits, co-pay, sub-limits and deductibles

- **Room rent limit:** a cap such as 1% of sum insured per day for a normal room. The danger is **proportionate deduction**: if the customer takes a room costing more than the cap, the insurer may reduce the *entire* claim, including surgeon, OT and nursing charges, in the same ratio.
- **Co-payment:** the customer pays a fixed share, such as 10% or 20%, of every claim. It lowers premium and is common in senior citizen plans. Make sure the customer chooses it knowingly.
- **Sub-limits:** caps on specific treatments or items, such as cataract per eye or a disease-wise cap. Ask which are present.
- **Deductible:** an amount the customer bears before the insurer pays. This is the structure behind a super top-up.

**Worked example of proportionate deduction.** Policy room limit Rs 5,000 a day, customer takes a Rs 10,000 a day room, and the total bill is Rs 4 lakh. Ratio 5,000/10,000 = 50%. The insurer may pay only 50% of the eligible bill, roughly Rs 2 lakh, leaving Rs 2 lakh with the customer. Recommend plans with **no room rent limit** where budget allows.

## 7. Super top-up and deductible

A **super top-up** pays once the combined claims in a year exceed the deductible, using the same or a separate policy as the base. A top-up pays only if a *single* claim exceeds the deductible; a **super** top-up counts claims **cumulatively** in the policy year. Pitch: "Your Rs 10 lakh base is the first line. The super top-up is a cheap second line for a big hospitalisation." It is typically the best value for customers with an employer cover, because the employer cover can serve as the deductible. [Verify: insurer wording on how group cover counts toward the deductible.]

## 8. Cashless versus reimbursement and network hospitals

- **Cashless:** the insurer settles directly with a **network hospital**. The customer pays only non-payable items. Pre-authorisation is needed for planned admissions; emergencies are normally intimated within 24 hours.
- **Reimbursement:** the customer pays and then submits bills and documents for refund. Available at any hospital, including non-network.
- Check that the customer's preferred hospitals, in their city and their parents' city, are in the network before the sale.
- Keep a list of **non-payable items** (consumables, administrative charges) so the customer is not surprised at discharge.
- IRDAI expects insurers to act on cashless authorisation and discharge within defined timelines. [Verify: current timelines.]

## 9. Portability, migration, no-claim bonus and restoration

- **Portability:** the customer can move to another insurer at renewal and retain **accrued continuity benefits** such as waiting-period credit, but only up to the previous sum insured. New cover above that is underwritten fresh. Apply well ahead of the renewal date. [Verify: current application timelines.]
- **Migration:** moving between plans of the same insurer, with similar credit.
- **No-claim bonus (NCB):** a sum insured increase, commonly 5% to 50% for each claim-free year, up to a cap. Confirm whether the premium rises or the bonus is "free".
- **Restoration (reset):** the sum insured is refilled after it is exhausted in a year. Check whether it applies to the same illness and to the same member.

## 10. Selling to young families, parents and senior citizens

- **Young families:** start early. Premiums are low, waiting periods run while the family is healthy, and a floater plus super top-up gives breadth. Add maternity cover only if planning a child, noting its own waiting period (commonly two to four years). [Verify.]
- **Parents above 55-60:** cover them on a separate plan. Consider a senior citizen plan, accept some co-pay to hold the premium, and check PED waiting periods and sub-limits carefully. A moderate base with a super top-up usually beats one very large, very costly base.
- **Tax and GST:** individual health insurance premiums are exempt from GST from 22 September 2025, which lowers the cost of cover. [Verify: current treatment, and the continued availability of Section 80D deductions depending on the tax regime chosen.]

## 11. Handling objections

- **"I have employer cover."** Ask: what happens when you change jobs, retire or are laid off? Group cover is typically tied to employment, has no personal continuity, and the sum insured is often modest. A personal plan, even a base of Rs 5 to 10 lakh, protects the family's continuity and waiting-period credit. Position the personal plan as the **foundation** and the employer cover as a bonus.
- **"Premium is too high."** Compare the annual premium with one hospitalisation in rupees. Offer levers: a higher deductible, a super top-up instead of a large base, co-pay, longer-term policies, or a smaller room category. Do not strip essentials such as the room rent limit just to hit a price.
- **"I never fall sick."** Acknowledge it, and then talk about accidents, infections and the fact that the cost of waiting is that waiting periods have not begun.

## 12. Claims support

The advisor's trust is built at claim time. Before the sale, explain the intimation steps and the documents needed. At the time of a claim, help with the helpline, TPA or insurer app, hospital pre-authorisation and discharge documents. After the claim, review whether the sum insured should be topped up. If a claim is rejected or delayed, guide the customer to the insurer's grievance channel and then the insurance ombudsman. [Verify: current grievance escalation steps.]

## 13. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Recommending the lowest premium without checking room rent limits, co-pay and sub-limits.
- Putting elderly parents on a young couple's floater, raising the premium and diluting cover.
- Quoting the sum insured without a bill-based justification.
- Not recording PED disclosure honestly, creating claim disputes later.
- Mixing up critical illness cover with hospitalisation cover.
- Ignoring network hospitals in the parents' city.
- Overpromising claim settlement. Describe the process, not a guarantee.

## 14. Practice questions

1. A customer has Rs 5 lakh employer cover and wants a cheap personal plan. What do you suggest? *(A Rs 5 to 10 lakh personal base, or a super top-up with the group cover as the deductible, so the family keeps continuity if the job changes.)*
2. The room rent limit is Rs 5,000 and the customer might choose a Rs 8,000 room. What is the risk? *(Proportionate deduction can reduce the whole claim, not just the room charge.)*
3. A customer wants to port to another insurer to raise cover from Rs 10 lakh to Rs 25 lakh. What happens to waiting-period credit? *(Credit carries over for the earlier Rs 10 lakh; the additional amount is underwritten fresh with its own waiting periods.)*
