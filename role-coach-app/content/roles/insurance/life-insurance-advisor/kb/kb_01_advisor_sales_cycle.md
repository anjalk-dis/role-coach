# The Advisor Sales Cycle

*Knowledge area: The advisor sales cycle. Audience: life insurance advisors (agents and POSPs) selling term and savings plans to individuals and families. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation against current IRDAI regulations and insurer processes.*

## 1. Why a sales cycle matters

Life insurance is bought rarely, understood poorly and postponed easily. Nobody wakes up wanting a policy. A good advisor therefore follows a repeatable cycle: **prospect, fact-find, recommend, handle objections, close, document, service, and ask for referrals.** Skipping a step shows up later as lapses, complaints or claim disputes.

The cycle is also the best protection for you. A customer who was asked proper questions, was given a clear recommendation and signed accurate forms is a customer who stays and refers.

## 2. Prospecting and referrals

Most successful advisors build on **warm markets**: family, friends, colleagues, neighbours, former classmates, society and community groups. Other good sources:

- **Referrals** from happy customers. Ask every customer for two names after the policy is issued, not before.
- **Centres of influence**: a chartered accountant, a doctor, a school principal, a housing society secretary.
- **Life events**: marriage, a new baby, a new home loan, a first job, a promotion, a new business.
- **Digital leads**: a short WhatsApp message with a useful tip works better than a brochure. Respect consent and the Do Not Disturb registry for calls and messages [Verify current TRAI rules].

Aim for a simple weekly target, for example 15 new conversations, 8 fact-finds and 3 proposals. The numbers matter less than the habit.

## 3. The fact-find (need analysis)

A fact-find is a conversation, not a form. Ask permission first: "May I ask a few questions about your family and plans, so I can suggest only what you need?"

Core questions:

1. **Family:** Who depends on your income? Spouse working? Children's ages? Parents?
2. **Income and expenses:** Annual income, monthly household expenses, savings habit.
3. **Liabilities:** Home loan, car loan, personal loan, outstanding amounts and tenure.
4. **Existing cover:** Any term plan, employer group cover, LIC or other policies, health insurance. Ask for the policy documents.
5. **Goals:** Child's education, marriage, retirement, buying a home, and by when.
6. **Health and habits:** Smoking, medical history, since these affect premium and underwriting. Never advise hiding them.

## 4. Sizing the cover

Two practical methods:

- **Income multiple:** a quick rule of thumb of 10 to 15 times annual income, depending on age and dependants.
- **Needs approach:** liabilities + future goals + income replacement for dependants, minus existing cover and liquid assets. This is more defensible.

**Worked example.** Rahul, 32, works in Pune, earns Rs 12 lakh a year. He has a Rs 40 lakh home loan, a wife who is a homemaker, a 2-year-old daughter and parents who depend on him. He has Rs 10 lakh of group cover from his employer, which ends if he changes jobs.

- Loan to be cleared: Rs 40 lakh
- Daughter's education and marriage goals: Rs 30 lakh
- Income replacement: Rs 8 lakh a year (family expenses) for 10 years = Rs 80 lakh
- Total need: Rs 1.5 crore
- Less liquid savings and personal cover: Rs 10 lakh
- **Recommended personal cover: about Rs 1.4 crore**, which is about 11.5 times his income, and fits the rule of thumb.

At his age and as a non-smoker, a Rs 1 crore to Rs 1.5 crore term plan to age 60 may cost roughly Rs 12,000 to Rs 20,000 a year, depending on insurer and health [Verify indicative premium with a current quote]. That is about Rs 1,000 to Rs 1,700 a month for protecting a Rs 1.4 crore family need. The advisor should show a real quote, not an estimate.

## 5. Presenting a recommendation simply

Recommend **one primary plan and at most one alternative**. Too many options cause delay.

Structure the presentation in four lines:

1. **What I heard:** "You want your wife and daughter to keep their life and the home loan cleared if something happens to you."
2. **What I recommend:** "A Rs 1.4 crore term plan until age 60, with critical illness rider if you want it."
3. **What it costs:** "About Rs X a year, which is Rs Y a month."
4. **What it does not do:** "It pays only if death occurs during the term. There is no maturity amount." Honest boundaries build trust.

Pitching to homemakers: when a spouse is a homemaker, do not say she has "no income". Explain the economic value of her work, such as childcare and household management, and the cost the family would bear to replace it. Where policy rules and underwriting allow, cover on a homemaker is usually smaller and linked to the working spouse's cover [Verify insurer's financial underwriting rules].

For savings plans, say clearly whether returns are **guaranteed or non-guaranteed**, the lock-in, the premium paying term, and what happens on surrender. Never promise a return that is not in the benefit illustration.

## 6. Handling objections

An objection is usually a request for information or reassurance. Listen, acknowledge, ask a question, then answer.

- **"It's too expensive."** "Compared with what? Let's look at Rs 1,200 a month against your Rs 1.4 crore family need." Then check whether cover or term can be adjusted.
- **"I'll think about it."** Ask: "Of course. What exactly would you like to think about, the amount, the premium, or whether you trust the company?" The real objection usually appears. Agree a specific next date.
- **"I don't believe in insurance. Claims are not paid."** Share the insurer's published claim settlement ratio [Verify latest IRDAI/insurer figures] and explain that claims are rejected mainly for non-disclosure, which is why accurate forms matter.
- **"I have cover from my employer."** Explain it usually ends when he leaves the job and is often small. It is a bonus, not a plan.
- **"I'll buy when I'm older."** Premium rises with age and health issues can make cover costly or unavailable.

## 7. Closing without pressure

Closing is the natural next step after a clear recommendation. Use a soft, direct question: "Shall we complete the form today so cover can start once the underwriting is done?" Avoid false urgency such as "this rate ends tomorrow" unless it is true and documented.

If the customer says no, thank them, leave a one-page summary and schedule a follow-up. Pressure now produces a lapse or a complaint later.

## 8. Proposal and documentation

- Complete the proposal form **with the customer**, in the customer's own words. Never fill in answers on their behalf without confirming them.
- Record **medical history, smoking, income and existing cover** accurately. Non-disclosure is the commonest reason for claim repudiation.
- Collect **KYC and income documents** as per insurer requirements, and arrange medical tests where required.
- Explain the **free-look period** (generally 15 days, 30 days for some distance-marketing cases [Verify]).
- Do not accept cash personally unless the insurer's process allows it. Prefer direct online or cheque payment to the insurer.

## 9. After-sales service and persistency

Business is only real when it renews. **Persistency** measures the share of policies still paying after 13 months, 25 months and later.

- Call after issuance, confirm the policy reached the customer, and explain the main terms.
- Set renewal reminders 30 days before due dates and help with auto-pay or ECS mandates.
- Review the policy once a year or on life events such as marriage, a child or a new loan.
- Support the family during a claim. That one act builds the strongest reputation.

## 10. Building a referral business

After service, ask: "Who else in your family or office should have this conversation?" Hand over two names with a quick introduction message. Thank referrers. A book built on 100 trusted customers will outperform one built on 500 pressured ones.

## 11. Common mistakes / coaching points

- Skipping the fact-find and pitching the product you earn the most on.
- Recommending a savings plan to a customer who mainly needs term cover.
- Filling the proposal form for the customer, leading to non-disclosure.
- Quoting returns, bonuses or "double your money" outside the approved illustration.
- Chasing the customer after a no, instead of agreeing a specific follow-up.
- Forgetting servicing, so the policy lapses at month 13.
- Criticising other insurers or advisors to win a case.

## 12. Practice questions

1. A customer says "I'll think about it." What is your first response? *(Acknowledge, then ask what exactly they want to think about, and agree a specific follow-up date.)*
2. Why is a Rs 10 lakh employer cover not enough for a person with a Rs 40 lakh home loan and two dependants? *(It is small relative to the need, and it ends when the job ends.)*
3. Your customer does not want to mention that he smokes. What do you do? *(Explain that non-disclosure can lead to claim rejection and that the form must be accurate. Do not submit it with false information.)*
