# Preparing for Your Appraisal

*Knowledge area: Preparing for your appraisal. Audience: employees (and managers coaching their teams). Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. How appraisal cycles work in Indian companies

Most Indian employers run one of two rhythms. **Annual cycles** follow the financial year (April to March) with reviews in April to June, or the calendar year with reviews in January to March. **Half-yearly cycles** add a mid-year check-in, often with a lighter form and sometimes a smaller increment or bonus decision. Many IT services firms and BFSI companies also use quarterly goal check-ins, even when the rating is annual. [Verify: the cycle dates, forms and tools for the learner's own company.]

A typical cycle has these steps:

1. **Goal setting** at the start of the year (KRAs, KPIs or OKRs).
2. **Mid-year review** to check progress and adjust goals.
3. **Self-assessment** by the employee.
4. **Manager review** with a proposed rating and comments.
5. **Calibration**, where managers compare ratings across teams.
6. **Final rating and increment or promotion decisions**, then communication to the employee.
7. **Feedback conversation** and next-cycle goals.

Coaching point: the self-assessment is your only formal chance to put your version of the year on record before the manager writes theirs. Treat it as a document that will be read by people who were not in the room.

## 2. KRAs, KPIs and OKRs

- **KRA (Key Result Area):** a broad area of responsibility, such as "customer retention" or "delivery quality".
- **KPI (Key Performance Indicator):** a number that tracks a KRA, such as "churn below 4%" or "defect leakage under 2%".
- **OKR (Objectives and Key Results):** an ambitious objective with 2 to 4 measurable key results, common in product and technology teams. OKRs are often *stretch* targets, so scoring 70% may be considered good. [Verify: whether the company ties OKR scores directly to ratings.]

Start your preparation by pulling out your goals sheet. Everything in your self-assessment should map back to it, or explain clearly why priorities changed during the year.

## 3. Build an evidence file through the year

Do not start collecting evidence in March. Keep a simple running document, a "brag file" or "work diary", and spend ten minutes at the end of each month adding to it.

What to capture:

- Delivered outcomes with numbers (revenue, cost, time, error rates, customer scores).
- Appreciation emails, client messages, Slack or Teams shout-outs (save screenshots or links, not just memory).
- Reports, dashboards and project closure notes.
- Stretch work outside your job description, such as covering for a colleague, mentoring a new joiner or leading a pilot.
- Learning: certifications, courses, internal talks.
- Setbacks and what you learned. These help you stay credible and are useful when the review discusses gaps.

Tip: record the *date*, *what you did*, *the result* and *who can vouch for it*. This makes your self-assessment faster and harder to dispute.

## 4. Writing achievement statements: STAR and impact-first

**STAR** stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result. It is a good way to think, but a self-assessment needs the **result first**. Reviewers skim. Lead with the impact, then add how you did it.

**Impact-first formula:** *Result (with number) + by doing what + for whom or in what context.*

### Before and after examples

| Before (vague) | After (impact-first) |
|---|---|
| "Worked on reducing customer complaints." | "Cut repeat complaints in the Pune retail branch by 28% (from 50 to 36 a month) by introducing a call-back checklist, improving CSAT from 3.9 to 4.3." |
| "Helped the team with automation." | "Automated the monthly MIS report, saving 12 hours a month across three analysts; the report now goes out two days earlier." |
| "Handled a difficult client." | "Retained a Rs 85 lakh annual account after a service failure by leading a recovery plan with weekly updates; the client renewed for two years." |
| "Participated in hiring." | "Interviewed 24 candidates and closed 6 hires for the QA team within 45 days, against a 60-day target." |

Notice the pattern: a number, a time frame, and a clear link to something the business cares about.

## 5. Quantifying impact when the work is not obviously numeric

Not every job produces a sales figure. Use one of these lenses:

- **Time:** hours saved, turnaround time reduced, delays avoided.
- **Money:** revenue won, cost saved, penalty avoided, budget kept under control.
- **Quality:** fewer errors, rework, audit observations or escalations.
- **Scale:** number of people trained, customers served, transactions handled.
- **Satisfaction:** CSAT, NPS, employee survey scores, stakeholder feedback.
- **Risk:** compliance issues avoided, incidents prevented.

If you cannot measure it exactly, **estimate honestly and say so**: "approximately 10 hours a month (based on my tracker)". Never inflate a number. An honest estimate with a method is more credible than a round figure with no source. [Verify: whether the company expects reviewers to validate numbers with data.]

## 6. Align to goals, values and the next level

Reviewers use three lenses: *what* you achieved (goals), *how* you achieved it (company values and behaviours), and *whether you operate at the next level* (for promotion). In your self-assessment:

- Group achievements under your KRAs or OKRs.
- Show one or two examples of values in action, such as collaboration, customer focus or integrity.
- If you want a promotion, show evidence of next-level work: larger scope, more independence, influence beyond your team.

## 7. 360 feedback

Some companies collect **360 feedback** from peers, direct reports, cross-functional partners and managers. It is usually anonymous or summarised.

How to use it:

- Choose reviewers who have seen your work closely, and who will be honest, not only friendly.
- Give them context: two or three projects to comment on.
- When you receive feedback, look for **patterns** across several people rather than reacting to a single comment.
- Mention themes in your self-assessment and say what you are doing about them. This shows maturity.

## 8. Ratings, calibration and the bell curve

Rating scales vary: 1 to 5, "Exceeds / Meets / Needs Improvement", or letter bands. A few key ideas:

- **Calibration:** managers and HR meet to compare ratings across teams so that "Exceeds" means the same in every team. Your manager may propose a rating that changes after calibration.
- **Normalisation or bell curve:** some companies expect ratings to follow a rough distribution, for example around 10% top, 70% middle and 20% below expectations. [Verify: many companies use guided ranges rather than strict forced distribution, and some have moved away from curves entirely.]
- **Why it matters:** you can do well and still receive a "Meets" rating because the team's top slots were limited. This is a system effect and not always a verdict on you.

Coaching point: ask your manager early in the year how calibration works at your company and what separates the rating bands.

## 9. How increments usually link to ratings (illustrative only)

Increments depend on the rating, the company's budget, your position in the salary band and sometimes the market. The numbers below are **purely illustrative** and not a prediction for any company.

| Rating | Illustrative increment range |
|---|---|
| Exceeds expectations | 12 to 18% |
| Meets expectations | 6 to 9% |
| Partially meets | 0 to 4% |

Illustrative worked example: Karthik, a software engineer in Chennai, earns Rs 12 lakh a year. His company has an overall increment budget of 8%. With a "Meets" rating and an illustrative 8% increment, his pay moves to Rs 12.96 lakh, a rise of Rs 96,000. A colleague rated "Exceeds" at an illustrative 15% would move from Rs 12 lakh to Rs 13.8 lakh. Karthik learns that the difference between the two is not only effort but also documented, higher-level impact. He therefore starts an evidence file in April and shares a one-page summary with his manager at the mid-year check-in.

## 10. Common mistakes / coaching points

- Writing the self-assessment the night before the deadline.
- Listing tasks ("responsible for...") instead of results.
- Using the same generic adjectives ("hardworking", "team player") with no evidence.
- Over-claiming team results as personal achievements. Say "I led" or "we delivered, my part was..." precisely.
- Ignoring gaps. Reviewers notice, and naming a gap with a plan builds trust.
- Assuming the manager remembers everything. Managers handle many people and remember recent events best.
- Comparing yourself with a colleague's rating instead of your own evidence and goals.

## 11. Practice questions

1. **Rewrite "Improved our onboarding process" in impact-first form.**
   *Example: "Cut new-joiner onboarding from 14 to 9 days by creating a checklist and buddy system, improving 30-day satisfaction scores from 3.6 to 4.2."*

2. **You cannot measure the exact benefit of a process change. What should you do?**
   *Estimate honestly, state the method (for example a tracker or sample), and mark it as an estimate. Do not inflate.*

3. **Why might your final rating differ from your manager's proposed rating?**
   *Calibration across teams and any distribution guidelines can adjust ratings so they are consistent company-wide.*
