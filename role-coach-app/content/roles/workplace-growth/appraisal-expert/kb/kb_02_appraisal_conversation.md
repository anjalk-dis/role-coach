# The Appraisal Conversation

*Knowledge area: The appraisal conversation. Audience: employees (and managers who want to understand the employee's side). Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. Prepare your talking points

The appraisal conversation is usually 30 to 60 minutes. You will not cover everything, so prepare a short, structured page:

1. **Three headline achievements** with numbers (from your evidence file).
2. **One or two growth areas** you have already identified, with what you are doing about them.
3. **Your ask**: a rating review, a raise, a promotion path, a new project, or support for learning.
4. **Two or three questions** for your manager, for example "What would take my performance to the next level?" and "What should I focus on in the next six months?"

Go in with the mindset of *collaborating on a shared picture of the year*, not winning an argument. Managers respond better to curiosity than to pressure.

## 2. Opening the conversation

Start with appreciation and agenda. A simple opening:

*"Thank you for making time. I have reflected on the year and prepared a few points. I would like to hear your view first, then share my highlights and talk about next year. Is that okay?"*

Letting the manager speak first lowers tension and tells you what you are dealing with. If the manager starts with the rating, listen to the whole explanation before responding. Take notes.

## 3. Discussing a rating you disagree with

A lower rating than expected is common and painful. A strong conversation has four moves.

**Move 1: Pause and listen.** Do not respond emotionally. Say: *"Thank you for sharing this. I would like to understand it better."*

**Move 2: Ask for specifics.** Vague feedback cannot be fixed. Ask:

- *"Which goals or behaviours drove this rating?"*
- *"Can you give me two examples where my work fell short of expectations?"*
- *"What would a rating one level higher have looked like for this role?"*

**Move 3: Share your evidence calmly.** Use facts, not feelings.

*"I would like to add some context. On the vendor migration, my plan was to finish in September; scope changed in July after the client asked for two extra modules. We still delivered by October 15 with zero priority-one defects. Could that be taken into account?"*

**Move 4: Agree next steps.** Even if the rating does not change, leave with clarity: *"If the rating stays as is, can we agree what I should do in the next six months to be rated higher, and set a check-in date?"*

Phrases to avoid: "This is unfair", "Everyone else got a better rating", "You never told me". Instead say: "I was surprised, and I would like to understand the gap."

### Escalation and appeal

Many companies have a route if you still disagree: a skip-level or HR conversation, a rating review committee or a formal grievance or appeal window. Some have none. [Verify: ask HR for the written appeal policy, time limits and what can be reviewed.] Use it respectfully:

- Wait 24 to 48 hours before deciding.
- Raise it first with your manager, then HR.
- Submit a short written note with facts and evidence.
- Do not discuss the matter negatively on team chats.

If you believe your rating was affected by discrimination, harassment or retaliation, speak to HR, the Internal Committee (for matters under the POSH Act) or a lawyer. That is a different process from a normal rating discussion.

## 4. Asking for a raise or promotion

**Timing.** The best time to plan is *before* the cycle closes, because budgets and promotion lists are usually fixed during calibration. Raise the topic at the mid-year check-in. If you ask only after final decisions, there may be little room to change anything. [Verify: company's promotion window and eligibility rules.]

**Build the case.**

- *Impact:* three or four achievements with numbers.
- *Scope:* things you now do that are bigger than your current role.
- *Market data:* salary ranges for similar roles from job postings, salary-survey reports and peers. Use ranges, not one number, and never claim data you do not have.
- *Fairness:* where you stand in your band. [Verify: whether the company shares salary bands.]

**Make the ask clearly.**

*"Over the last year I led the payments dashboard project that reduced reconciliation time by 40%, and I now mentor two junior analysts. Based on this scope and what I see in the market for similar roles, I would like to be considered for a promotion to Senior Analyst. What would you need to see from me to support this?"*

**If the answer is no or "not now".** Ask: *"What are the specific gaps?"* *"What timeline is realistic?"* *"Can we put this in my development plan?"*

**Alternatives when a raise is not possible.** Bigger scope or a stretch project, a revised title, a learning budget or certification support, flexible working, an off-cycle review in six months, or a variable pay discussion.

### Worked example

Meera, a customer success manager in Bengaluru earning Rs 18 lakh, was rated "Meets" and offered a 7% increment (Rs 1.26 lakh, new pay Rs 19.26 lakh). She believed her work justified more. She prepared: her account renewals were up from 82% to 91%, she had trained four new CSMs, and her market research showed similar roles at Rs 20 to 23 lakh. In the meeting she said: "I appreciate the rating. I would like to talk about how my scope has grown. Here are the numbers. Could we discuss a path to Rs 20 lakh or beyond, either now or at a mid-year review?" Her manager could not change the increment mid-cycle but agreed to move her to a lead role from October with a mid-year pay review and to fund a customer success certification. Meera got a timeline, a clear role change and written follow-up, which was better than a rushed "no". [Illustrative example only.]

## 5. Receiving tough feedback without defensiveness

Defensiveness is natural: your face gets hot, you want to explain. A simple routine:

1. **Breathe and listen to the end.** Do not interrupt.
2. **Repeat what you heard.** *"So the concern is that my updates reach stakeholders late. Is that right?"*
3. **Ask for examples.** *"Could you share a recent instance?"*
4. **Separate facts from tone.** Even harshly delivered feedback may contain something useful.
5. **Say thank you.** *"I appreciate you telling me this directly."*
6. **Propose one action.** *"I will send a weekly status mail by Friday noon. Can we review it in a month?"*

You do not have to agree to everything on the spot. It is acceptable to say: *"I would like some time to think about this and come back tomorrow."*

## 6. PIPs: what they are and how to respond

A **Performance Improvement Plan (PIP)** is a structured plan, often 30, 60 or 90 days, that sets clear expectations, support and review points when performance falls short. Its intent may be to help someone recover, but it can also precede an exit. A PIP is not automatically the end. [Verify: company PIP policy, duration, support offered and outcomes.]

How to respond constructively:

- **Stay calm and ask for the PIP in writing** with specific, measurable goals and dates.
- **Clarify success criteria**: "What exactly will count as meeting the plan?"
- **Ask what support is available**: training, a mentor, reduced distractions, regular check-ins.
- **Agree on weekly or fortnightly reviews** and keep your own notes.
- **Deliver the work**, and share evidence regularly.
- **Look after yourself.** A PIP is stressful. Use EAP or trusted friends, and speak to HR if you feel the plan is unfair or unrealistic. For legal questions, consult a lawyer.

## 7. Follow-up email summary

Within 24 hours, send a brief, neutral email to confirm what was discussed.

*"Hi Rahul, thank you for the conversation today. My summary: (1) my rating is Meets Expectations; (2) you will check whether the vendor migration scope change can be noted in my record; (3) I will lead the Q2 dashboard project and share a monthly update; (4) we will meet on 15 July to review progress and discuss a promotion path. Please let me know if I have missed anything."*

This protects both sides, turns talk into commitments and creates a record.

## 8. Setting next-cycle goals (SMART)

Good goals are **S**pecific, **M**easurable, **A**chievable, **R**elevant and **T**ime-bound.

| Weak goal | SMART version |
|---|---|
| "Improve communication." | "Send a weekly status update to the project sponsor every Friday for the next two quarters; get at least 4/5 on stakeholder feedback in September." |
| "Learn cloud." | "Complete the AWS Solutions Architect Associate certification by 30 November and apply it to one migration proposal by March." |
| "Reduce errors." | "Reduce rework in monthly reports from 8 corrections to 3 by September through a peer-check step." |

Aim for **3 to 5 goals**, include at least one growth goal and agree on check-in dates.

## 9. Common mistakes / coaching points

- Arriving with no evidence or only emotions.
- Comparing yourself to named colleagues.
- Threatening to resign as a negotiation tactic. This can backfire.
- Asking for a raise with no market data or impact case.
- Leaving without written next steps.
- Taking a rating personally. It is a judgement on one year of work, not on your worth.

## 10. Practice questions

1. **Your manager gives a rating lower than you expected. What is your first question?**
   *Ask for specifics: which goals or behaviours drove the rating and what examples support it.*

2. **Name two alternatives if a raise is not possible now.**
   *Bigger scope or a title change, a learning budget, or an off-cycle review in six months.*

3. **What should you ask for first if you are placed on a PIP?**
   *The plan in writing, with specific measurable goals, dates, support and review points.*
