# Giving Fair Reviews as a Manager

*Knowledge area: Giving fair reviews as a manager. Audience: managers and team leads (and employees who want to understand the manager's side). Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. Continuous feedback beats once-a-year surprises

The most common employee complaint about appraisals is: "Nobody told me this during the year." If the annual review contains news, the process has already failed.

A fair review is the **summary of conversations already held**. Practical rhythm:

- **Weekly or fortnightly one-on-ones** (20 to 30 minutes) to discuss priorities, blockers and feedback.
- **Real-time feedback** within a day or two of an event, for both good and poor work.
- **Quarterly check-ins** to review goals and note progress in writing.
- **Mid-year review** to say plainly where the person is heading: "At this rate, I expect a rating around Meets. Here is what would move it."

Coaching point: if the first time a team member hears about a concern is in the appraisal meeting, the manager owns part of that outcome.

## 2. Common rating biases

Every manager is biased. Awareness and process reduce harm.

| Bias | What it looks like | Antidote |
|---|---|---|
| **Recency** | The last two months outweigh the first ten. | Keep running notes; review the whole year's evidence. |
| **Halo / horns** | One strong (or weak) trait colours everything else. | Rate each goal separately before giving an overall rating. |
| **Central tendency** | Everyone gets "Meets" to avoid conflict. | Use evidence to separate high and low performers. |
| **Leniency (or strictness)** | Ratings are consistently too generous or too harsh. | Compare against the rating definitions and calibration. |
| **Similarity / affinity** | Higher ratings for people who share your background, college, language or style. | Ask: "Would I rate this the same if the person were different from me?" |
| **Gender and parental bias** | Women, new mothers or caregivers seen as less committed; leave periods unfairly counted against them; men and women described with different words. | Rate outcomes, not presence or hours; adjust for approved leave; check wording. |
| **Proximity bias** | People in the office or in your time zone get more credit than remote workers. | Track contributions through outcomes, not visibility. |

Many Indian workplaces also have regional, language, college-brand and "old boys' network" biases. Name them in calibration and challenge them. [Verify: whether the company has unconscious-bias training or review checklists.]

## 3. Giving feedback with SBI

**SBI** stands for **Situation, Behaviour, Impact**. It keeps feedback specific, observable and non-judgemental.

- **Situation:** when and where.
- **Behaviour:** what you saw or heard, without interpreting motives.
- **Impact:** the effect on the team, customer or work.

| Weak | SBI version |
|---|---|
| "You are careless." | "In Tuesday's client call (S), the figures on slide 6 did not match the report (B). The client questioned our numbers and we lost ten minutes of the meeting (I)." |
| "You are a great team player." | "During the March release (S), you stayed to help two colleagues test their modules (B). We shipped on time and the QA lead said defect counts dropped (I)." |

After SBI, **ask**: "How did you see it?" Feedback is a conversation, not a verdict.

## 4. Writing a fair, evidence-based review

Use a simple process:

1. **Gather evidence** for the whole year: goal sheets, project results, customer feedback, peer inputs, your own notes and the employee's self-assessment.
2. **Read the self-assessment first.** It may reveal work you did not see.
3. **Rate each goal or competency** against clear definitions before choosing an overall rating.
4. **Write specific comments**: facts, impact and one development suggestion.
5. **Check for bias**: swap names mentally, check your wording and compare with colleagues' reviews.
6. **Avoid comparing the person to peers in the comments.** The review is against expectations for the role.

Weak comment: "Ravi is a good performer but needs to improve communication."
Better: "Ravi delivered the loan-origination integration two weeks early, saving about Rs 4 lakh in contractor costs. In two sprint reviews, status updates arrived after the deadline, so the product owner had to chase. Next year, a weekly update by Thursday noon would help."

## 5. Calibration meetings

In calibration, managers present proposed ratings, and HR or senior leaders challenge them for consistency. Done well, calibration reduces bias. Done poorly, it becomes a negotiation about who argues best or who gets the "slots".

How to prepare:

- Bring **evidence**: three to five specific examples per person, especially for the highest and lowest ratings.
- Be ready to say what the person did that is **above or below** the role's expectation.
- **Defend your people fairly**, but accept the data.
- Watch for **forced fit**: if the guidance suggests only 10% can be in the top band, make sure the *right* people are in it, and say so if the guidance leaves a genuinely strong performer out. [Verify: company distribution guidelines and flexibility.]
- After calibration, **explain changes honestly**. Never blame HR for something you did not argue for.

## 6. Delivering a low rating with empathy

Plan the meeting: private room, enough time, no interruptions. Share the rating early and do not bury it.

A structure:

1. **Open directly and kindly:** "I want to talk about your rating this year. It is Partially Meets Expectations. I would like to explain how I got there and what we can do together."
2. **Give two or three specific examples** (SBI), not a long list.
3. **Acknowledge strengths** that are genuinely true. Do not use them as a sandwich to hide the message.
4. **Ask for their view** and listen.
5. **Explain the consequences honestly** (increment, promotion timing), and say what you do not know. Do not promise things you cannot control.
6. **Agree on a plan**: two or three goals, support, check-ins.

## 7. Handling emotional reactions

People may cry, go silent, get angry or argue. Stay calm.

- **Pause.** Offer water or a short break. "Take a moment. We can continue when you are ready."
- **Acknowledge:** "I can see this is hard to hear."
- **Do not rush to fix or reverse the rating** under pressure.
- **Stay on facts.** "Let me show you the examples and then hear your side."
- **Offer to continue later** if needed, and follow up in writing.
- If the person appears **highly distressed**, suggest the Employee Assistance Programme (EAP) and check in later. In a crisis, call 112 or Tele-MANAS 14416.

## 8. Underperformance and PIPs, fairly

Before a PIP, ask yourself:

- Did I set **clear expectations** and tell the person early and in writing?
- Did I offer **support** (training, tools, mentoring, workload adjustments)?
- Is the problem **skill, will, or situation**? A new role, personal difficulty, unclear goals or too much work needs different action than low effort.
- Am I treating this person the **same as others** in similar situations?

If a PIP is needed: involve HR, give written, measurable goals, a realistic 30/60/90-day window, regular reviews and honest feedback. A PIP should offer a real chance to improve. [Verify: company policy and any legal requirements before termination decisions.]

## 9. Goal-setting and development plans

For next year:

- Agree **3 to 5 SMART goals** linked to team priorities.
- Include **one development goal** based on the person's strengths and aspirations.
- Use a simple development plan: *skill to build, how (course, shadowing, stretch project), by when, how we will measure progress.*
- Record the plan and review it quarterly.

## 10. Documentation

Good documentation protects fairness. Keep factual notes after one-on-ones: date, topic, feedback given, agreed actions. Do not record gossip or personal comments. Store notes as the company's policy requires. [Verify: data privacy and retention rules.] The review should never contain a surprise that is not in your notes.

### Worked example

Anita manages a team of eight at an Indian IT services firm in Pune. In March, she drafts ratings and notices that Deepak, who sits next to her and shares her college background, has been given "Exceeds", while remote team member Farhana, who led a client migration saving Rs 22 lakh a year, is at "Meets". She checks her notes: Deepak delivered well on routine tasks; Farhana's project closed in October and she had barely mentioned it since. Anita suspects **similarity** and **proximity** bias. She reviews both against the rating definitions, writes three specific examples each and decides to propose Farhana for "Exceeds" and Deepak for "Meets plus". At calibration, she presents the examples, and the panel agrees, giving Farhana an increment of 14% rather than 8% (an illustrative difference of about Rs 1.1 lakh on a Rs 18 lakh salary). Anita starts monthly notes for the whole team next year. [Illustrative example only.]

## 11. Common mistakes / coaching points

- Surprising people in the annual review.
- Rating on personality, not outcomes.
- Copying last year's comments or relying on memory.
- Giving everyone "Meets" to avoid difficult conversations.
- Using vague words such as "attitude problem" with no examples.
- Promising an increment or promotion before approvals.
- Treating calibration as a bargaining contest.
- Starting a PIP with no earlier feedback.

## 12. Practice questions

1. **What is SBI, and why use it?**
   *Situation, Behaviour, Impact. It keeps feedback specific, observable and less personal, so the person can act on it.*

2. **Your strongest performer is your friend. How do you reduce bias?**
   *Rate each goal against definitions, use evidence and compare with colleagues' ratings. Ask whether you would give the same rating to someone you do not know well.*

3. **A team member cries when given a low rating. What do you do?**
   *Pause, acknowledge the feeling, offer a break, stay on facts, do not reverse under pressure, follow up in writing, and suggest EAP if they seem highly distressed.*
