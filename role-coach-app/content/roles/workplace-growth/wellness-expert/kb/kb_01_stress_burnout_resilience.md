# Stress, Burnout and Resilience

*Knowledge area: Stress, burnout and resilience. Audience: employees at any level who want to manage stress and build healthier work habits. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. Stress and burnout are not the same thing

**Stress** is the body and mind responding to demands. A short burst is normal and can even help: a deadline, a client presentation, a difficult conversation. Stress usually feels like *too much*: too many demands, too little time, a racing mind. When the demand passes and the person rests, they tend to recover.

**Burnout** is different. The World Health Organization's ICD-11 describes burnout as an **occupational phenomenon**, not a medical condition, resulting from chronic workplace stress that has not been successfully managed. [Verify: exact current WHO wording.] It is usually described through three dimensions:

- **Exhaustion:** feeling drained, with rest not fully restoring energy.
- **Cynicism or distance:** feeling negative, detached or resentful about the job.
- **Reduced effectiveness:** feeling less capable, making more mistakes, struggling to concentrate.

A simple way to explain the difference aloud: stress feels like too much, burnout feels like not enough left. Stress is the engine running hot; burnout is the tank running empty.

As a coach, you never tell someone "you have burnout". You can say that what they describe sounds like it matches some common early signs, and that a doctor or the EAP can look at it properly.

## 2. Early signs worth noticing

Early signs are easy to dismiss as "just a busy phase". Encourage people to watch for a pattern that lasts more than a couple of weeks.

- **Body:** constant tiredness, headaches, tight shoulders, disturbed sleep.
- **Mood:** irritability, feeling flat, dreading Monday.
- **Behaviour:** skipping lunch, working late but achieving less, withdrawing from the team.
- **Attitude:** "what is the point", sarcasm about the company.

A useful self-check: *Am I more tired than a good weekend can fix? More negative about work than before? Less effective, despite working harder?* Two or three "yes" answers over several weeks is a reason to act, not to panic.

## 3. Quick resets you can do at your desk

These are short, general relaxation techniques. They are optional and should always be offered gently. If someone feels dizzy or uncomfortable, they should stop and breathe normally.

**Box breathing (about two minutes).** Spoken step by step:

1. Sit comfortably with your feet on the floor and your shoulders relaxed.
2. Breathe out gently first.
3. Breathe in through your nose for a slow count of four.
4. Hold gently for a count of four.
5. Breathe out slowly for a count of four.
6. Pause for a count of four.
7. Repeat this four to six times. If a count of four feels too long, use three.

**5-4-3-2-1 grounding (about two minutes).** This brings attention back to the present when the mind is racing.

1. Look around and name **five** things you can see.
2. Notice **four** things you can feel, such as your feet on the floor or the chair against your back.
3. Notice **three** things you can hear.
4. Notice **two** things you can smell, or two things you like in the room.
5. Name **one** slow breath you can take right now, and take it.

**A short walk (three to five minutes).** Stand up, leave the screen, and walk to the pantry or the balcony. Leave the phone behind if you can. A change of place often resets the mind better than pushing on.

## 4. Having a workload conversation with your manager

Many people suffer quietly because they do not know how to raise a concern. A good conversation is specific, calm and solution-focused. Prepare three things: the facts (what is on your plate), the impact (what is slipping), and the options (what you propose).

A short script:

*"I would like ten minutes to talk about my workload. Right now I am handling the client migration, the weekly reports and two escalations. I am worried the quality is slipping and I am working until ten most nights. Can we look at priorities together? I can hand over the weekly reports, or we could move one deadline by a week. What would work best from your side?"*

Coaching points:

- Choose a calm moment, not the middle of a crisis.
- Use facts and examples, not "everything is too much".
- Offer options so the manager can say yes to something.
- Follow up in writing. If it does not help, try HR or the EAP.

## 5. Boundaries that protect energy

Boundaries are about doing sustainable work, not less work.

- **Time boundaries:** decide a realistic finishing time most days and say it out loud to the team.
- **Availability boundaries:** agree when you will respond to messages. Not every message needs an instant reply.
- **Task boundaries:** when asked to take something on, say "Let me check my priorities and come back to you by 4 pm" rather than agreeing automatically.
- **Saying no kindly:** "I cannot take this on this week without dropping something else. Which would you like me to move?"

In many Indian workplaces, saying no to a senior feels uncomfortable. Framing a boundary as a question about priorities is often easier.

## 6. Recovery is part of performance

Resilience is not about being tough all the time. It is the ability to recover after pressure. Recovery needs both small daily habits and bigger breaks.

- **Daily:** a proper lunch away from the screen and a clear end to the workday.
- **Weekly:** at least one genuinely switched-off evening.
- **Periodically:** using leave. A proper holiday is not weak commitment.

Encourage the person to choose what actually refreshes them: music, a hobby, time with family, a reflective practice.

## 7. Worked example: the IT employee on late US calls

Karthik is a 29-year-old software engineer in Bengaluru working with a US client. He has calls from 8:30 pm to 10 pm three nights a week and still starts at 10 am the next day. For six weeks he has been tired, short with his flatmates, skipping dinner until 11 pm and scrolling his phone in bed. At work he feels he is "just ticking boxes" and has started dreading the client calls.

How a coach might respond:

1. **Acknowledge:** "Six weeks of late calls and tired mornings is genuinely draining. It makes sense that you feel flat."
2. **Notice the pattern, without labelling:** exhaustion, feeling distant from the work and lower effectiveness are all common early warning signs. Say that these deserve attention, without saying he has burnout.
3. **Small reset first:** a five-minute walk before the first call, box breathing before the call starts, and a light dinner before 8 pm instead of 11 pm.
4. **Protect recovery:** ask for a later start after late calls, and keep a wind-down with no phone for the last 20 minutes before sleep.
5. **Manager conversation:** use the script in section 4. Propose rotating the late calls with a teammate, or limiting them to two nights a week.
6. **Know the next step:** if the exhaustion and low mood continue for more than two weeks even after these changes, suggest speaking to a doctor or the company EAP.

## 8. When to go beyond self-help

Self-help has limits. Suggest a doctor or the company EAP when:

- tiredness, low mood, anxiety or poor sleep last more than about two weeks and are not improving;
- symptoms are affecting work, relationships or daily functioning;
- the person has physical symptoms they are worried about, such as chest pain, constant headaches or palpitations;
- they say they feel hopeless, trapped or that nothing matters.

If a person mentions self-harm, suicide, wanting to disappear or being unsafe, coaching stops. The coach responds warmly and briefly, urges them to contact emergency services on **112** or **Tele-MANAS on 14416** right now [Verify], and encourages them to reach a trusted person immediately.

## 9. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Telling someone they "have burnout" or "are depressed". Coaches do not diagnose.
- Treating exhaustion as only a time-management problem when workload may be the cause.
- Offering a breathing exercise as a cure-all. Resets do not fix chronic overload.
- Pushing an exercise on someone who finds it uncomfortable. Always keep it optional.
- Waiting for a crisis. Early small actions work better than late big ones.

## 10. Practice questions

1. What is the difference between stress and burnout in plain words? *(Stress feels like too much, with demands outrunning capacity but with recovery possible. Burnout is the result of long-running, unmanaged workplace stress: exhaustion, cynicism and reduced effectiveness.)*
2. A colleague says "I think I have burnout." How should the coach respond? *(Do not confirm or deny a diagnosis. Acknowledge the feeling, explore the pattern, offer small steps, and suggest a doctor or the EAP if it persists.)*
3. What are the three parts of a good workload conversation with a manager? *(The facts, the impact and the options you propose.)*
