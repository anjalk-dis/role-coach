# Supporting Others and Seeking Help

*Knowledge area: Supporting others and seeking help. Audience: employees at any level who want to manage stress and build healthier work habits. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. Why peer support matters

Most people who struggle at work do not announce it. A colleague, a friend or a team member is often the first person to notice. You do not need to be a counsellor to help. You need to be kind, curious and willing to point towards proper support. This is general guidance for everyday workplace situations, not a clinical protocol. Your role is to **notice, ask, listen and encourage**, not to diagnose, treat or solve.

## 2. Noticing when a colleague is struggling

Look for a **change from their usual self**, lasting more than a few days. No single sign proves anything.

- **Work:** missed deadlines, unusual mistakes, trouble concentrating, arriving late or leaving abruptly, taking lots of unplanned leave.
- **Behaviour:** withdrawing from team lunches, going quiet in meetings, snapping at others, or looking tearful.
- **Appearance and energy:** looking exhausted, unwell or unusually unkempt, or constantly complaining of headaches or tiredness.
- **Words:** "I cannot cope", "what is the point", "I am a burden", "everyone would be better off".

If you hear words that suggest hopelessness, or any talk of self-harm, take them seriously. See section 8.

## 3. The Notice-Ask-Listen-Encourage model

A simple four-step model that can be spoken aloud:

1. **Notice.** Pick up the change. Choose a private, unhurried moment, away from the open floor, such as a coffee break or a walk.
2. **Ask.** Start with care and an observation, not a judgement. "I have noticed you have been quieter lately. How are you really doing?" Use open questions and give them time.
3. **Listen.** Let them talk. Do not interrupt, fix or compare. Reflect back what you heard: "That sounds exhausting." Silence is fine.
4. **Encourage.** Gently encourage support. "Would it help to talk to someone, like your doctor or the EAP? I can help you find the number." Agree a small next step and offer to check in again in a few days.

If they say "I'm fine", accept it and leave the door open: "Okay. If that changes, I am around."

## 4. What not to say

Well-meant words can close a conversation. Try to avoid:

- "Cheer up", "Be positive", "Everyone gets stressed."
- "At least you have a good job." This dismisses their experience.
- "You should just..." Advice too early feels like judgement.
- "I know exactly how you feel." You may not, and the focus shifts to you.
- "You must have depression/burnout." Never diagnose.
- Comparing their situation with someone else's.
- Gossiping about it afterwards. Keep what you hear respectful and do not share it casually.

Better phrases: "Thank you for telling me." "That sounds really hard." "You do not have to go through this alone." "What would help right now?"

## 5. Encouraging professional help

Help-seeking can feel difficult in India because of stigma, fear of being judged, or worry about job impact. Normalise it: seeing a doctor or counsellor is like seeing a doctor for a fever. It is a sensible step.

- Offer options: their family doctor, a mental health professional, the company EAP, or a helpline.
- Offer practical help: finding the number, sitting with them while they call, or covering a meeting so they can attend an appointment.
- Do not force it. Respect their pace, but encourage again later if the situation does not improve.
- If you are worried about immediate safety, do not wait. Follow the crisis steps in section 8.

## 6. Managers' responsibilities

Managers have an additional duty of care. They are not clinicians, but they set the culture.

- **Know the signs and the support available.** Keep EAP details handy.
- **Hold regular one-to-ones** that ask about workload and wellbeing, not only tasks.
- **Act on workload.** If someone is overloaded, change the load, deadlines or priorities. Encouraging yoga does not fix a workload problem.
- **Be consistent and fair,** and avoid singling out someone in front of the team.
- **Protect privacy.** Share only what is needed, with the person's knowledge, and involve HR where policy requires it. Do not promise absolute confidentiality, since some situations require escalation for safety.
- **Model healthy behaviour:** take leave, avoid sending late-night messages, and speak openly about balance.
- **Follow up.** After a first conversation, check in again.

## 7. What is an EAP?

An **Employee Assistance Programme (EAP)** is a confidential support service offered by many employers, usually run by an external provider. ## 8. Crisis protocol

If someone mentions self-harm, suicide, wanting to disappear, or being unsafe, **this is an emergency response, not a coaching moment.**

1. **Stay calm and stay with them.** Listen without judging. Do not leave them alone if you believe they are in immediate danger.
2. **Ask directly and kindly.** "Are you thinking of harming yourself?" Asking does not plant the idea.
3. **Get help right now.** Call the emergency number **112**, or contact **Tele-MANAS on 14416**, India's national tele mental health helpline. [Verify: current number, hours and languages.]
4. **Involve a trusted person.** A family member, close friend, manager or HR, depending on the situation. Safety comes first.
5. **Do not promise to keep it secret.** Be honest: "I care about you, and I need to get you support."
6. **Follow up** and look after yourself afterwards.

## 9. Looking after yourself as a supporter

Supporting others can be heavy. You are not responsible for fixing anyone.

- Set limits on what you can offer, such as a short chat now and a proper conversation tomorrow.
- Do not become someone's only support. Encourage wider help.
- Talk to someone you trust, without sharing identifying details unnecessarily, or use the EAP yourself.
- Keep up your own routines of sleep, movement and breaks.
- Notice your own warning signs, such as feeling drained or dreading the conversations.

## 10. Worked example: checking in with a quiet teammate

Priya, a team lead in a Hyderabad shared-services centre, notices that Arjun, a usually cheerful analyst, has missed two deadlines, skips team lunch and looks tired. She asks him for a short tea break.

- **Notice and ask:** "Arjun, I noticed you have seemed a bit quiet lately, and I wanted to check in. How are things with you?"
- **Listen:** He says he is not sleeping, his father is unwell in his hometown and he feels he is letting everyone down. Priya says, "That sounds like a lot to carry. Thank you for telling me." She does not rush to fix it.
- **Encourage:** "Would it help to talk to someone? Our EAP has confidential counselling, and I can share the number. We could also look at your deadlines this week." They agree to move one deadline and she sends the EAP details.
- **Follow up:** She checks in two days later, and asks whether a doctor visit might help if sleep stays poor.
- **Boundary:** She does not diagnose him or share the story with the wider team. She tells him that if he ever feels unsafe, she will help him contact emergency support.

## 11. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Rushing to give advice before listening.
- Saying "I won't tell anyone" when safety may require action.
- Ignoring the signs because "it is not my place".
- Trying to be the counsellor instead of connecting the person to professional help.
- Treating one hard week as a crisis, or a long-standing change as just a mood.
- Forgetting to follow up.

## 12. Practice questions

1. What are the four steps of the Notice-Ask-Listen-Encourage model? *(Notice the change, ask with care, listen without fixing, encourage support and follow up.)*
2. A colleague says they want to disappear. What do you do? *(Treat it as an emergency: stay with them, encourage contacting 112 or Tele-MANAS 14416 right away, involve a trusted person, and do not promise secrecy.)*
3. Why should you avoid saying "You must have depression"? *(Only a qualified professional can diagnose. Labelling can scare or alienate the person. Encourage a doctor or the EAP instead.)*
