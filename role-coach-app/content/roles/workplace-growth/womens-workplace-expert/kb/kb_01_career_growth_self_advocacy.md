# Career Growth and Self-Advocacy

*Knowledge area: Career growth and self-advocacy. Audience: women professionals at any career stage, and the managers and allies who support them. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. Why self-advocacy matters

Good work does not always speak for itself. Managers are busy, projects are shared, and decisions about pay, promotion and stretch roles are often made in rooms where the employee is absent. Self-advocacy means making your contribution, your goals and your next step clear to the people who decide. It is a learnable skill, not a personality trait, and it can be done in a style that suits you.

Many people are taught to value modesty and teamwork. Both are strengths, but they can leave individual impact unrecorded. The aim is not to become louder; it is to become easier to notice and easier to back. Organisations carry their own responsibility for fair processes, and managers can do a lot to make advocacy unnecessary.

## 2. Building visibility

Visibility is the gap between the value you create and the value decision-makers know about. Practical ways to close it:

- **Keep a "wins" log.** One line per week: what you did, who benefited, and a number if possible (hours saved, revenue, error rate).
- **Send short progress notes.** A three-line update to your manager and key stakeholders after a milestone is ordinary professional hygiene, not boasting.
- **Say "we" for the team and "I" for your decisions.** "We shipped the migration; I designed the rollback plan and ran the cutover."
- **Widen the circle.** Have a coffee or a call each month with someone outside your immediate team: another function, a senior leader, an alumna.

## 3. Mentorship versus sponsorship

A **mentor** advises you. A **sponsor** advocates for you when you are not in the room. Both matter, but promotions are usually won through sponsors.

| | Mentor | Sponsor |
|---|---|---|
| Main role | Gives advice, perspective, feedback | Puts your name forward, spends influence |
| Seniority | Any level with relevant experience | Senior, with a seat at decision tables |
| Typical ask | "How would you approach this?" | "Would you support me for the lead role on X?" |
| Basis | Rapport and trust | Trust in your results and potential |

Sponsors are earned through visible performance and a specific ask. Coaching tip: do not ask a senior person to "be my sponsor" cold. Instead, deliver something useful to them, share your goal in one sentence, and ask for one concrete action, such as an introduction or a recommendation for a project.

## 4. Preparing a promotion case

Start 6 to 9 months before the cycle, not the week of the review. Build the case on evidence:

1. **Scope of the next role.** Read the job description or grade framework one level up. List which expectations you already meet.
2. **Evidence.** For each expectation, note two examples with outcomes, numbers and stakeholders.
3. **Feedback.** Collect 3 to 4 short written endorsements from peers, clients or partner teams.
4. **Gaps.** Name one or two honestly, with a plan. This builds credibility.
5. **The ask.** Request a dedicated conversation: "I would like to discuss what it would take to be considered for the senior manager role this cycle. Can we set up 30 minutes?"
6. **Follow up.** Confirm outcomes in writing and agree on a timeline.

Frame the conversation around the business: "I have already been running the vendor relationship and mentoring two juniors; formalising the role would let me take on the regional rollout."

## 5. Pay negotiation

**Know your market.** Use several sources: salary surveys, recruiter conversations, peers in your professional network, and public job postings that disclose ranges. Triangulate; no single source is reliable. [Verify: pay transparency expectations differ by company and are not currently mandated for private employers in India.]

**Anchor with a range whose bottom is your target.** If your target is Rs 24 lakh, you might say "Based on the scope and market data, I am looking at Rs 24 to 27 lakh." The first number said often shapes the conversation.

**Make the ask clearly.** State the number, then stop talking. Silence is part of negotiation.

**Look at the whole package.** Fixed pay, variable pay, bonus, ESOPs, learning budget, flexibility, title and review date.

**Handling "there is no budget":**

- Ask what the process is: "When is the next compensation cycle, and what would I need to show to be at the top of it?"
- Ask for a dated commitment: "Can we agree to revisit in October with a target of Rs 26 lakh?"
- Propose non-cash options: a title change, extra leave, a learning allowance, a flexible work arrangement.

## 6. Worked example: the first negotiation

Priya, a product analyst in Pune, earns Rs 18 lakh per year after three years. She has led a pricing dashboard that her manager says saved the sales team about 6 hours a week, and she now mentors two new joiners. Market conversations suggest analysts at her level with her scope earn Rs 22 to 26 lakh.

Her plan: she books a meeting with her manager and says, "I would like to talk about my compensation. With the pricing work and mentoring, and what I am seeing in the market, I am looking at Rs 23 to 25 lakh." The manager replies, "We do not have budget until April." Priya answers, "I understand. Can we agree to a revision to Rs 23 lakh effective April, confirmed in writing now, and meanwhile give me ownership of the next dashboard project and the Senior Analyst title?" She leaves with a documented path rather than a vague promise.

Coaching points: she named a number, tied it to business value, and converted "no budget" into a dated plan with a non-cash gain.

## 7. Interruptions and credit-taking

When you are interrupted or someone repeats your idea as their own, quick, calm phrases work better than silence or confrontation.

- **Reclaiming the floor:** "Let me finish my point, then I would love your view."
- **Credit-taking in the moment:** "Thanks for building on the idea I shared earlier. To add to it..."
- **After the fact, privately:** "I noticed the analysis I prepared was presented without my name. Going forward, I would like us to acknowledge each contributor."
- **Pre-empt it:** send the idea in writing before the meeting, or open with "I will share three points."

**Managers and allies** can amplify: "Anita raised this earlier; Anita, please continue." They can also set meeting norms, such as no interruptions during the first answer, and track who speaks.

## 8. Office housework and saying no

"Office housework" is the non-promotable work: note-taking, party planning, onboarding buddies, filling in for absent colleagues. It is useful, but if it falls disproportionately on some people, it eats the time that leads to promotion.

Responses:

- **Rotate.** "Let us make a rota for minutes so it is fair."
- **Trade.** "I can take notes this time if someone else covers the next two meetings."
- **Say no with an alternative.** "I cannot take this on while delivering the audit. I can help set up the template."
- **Ask the manager to prioritise.** "I have X, Y, Z. Which should I drop?"

## 9. Impostor feelings

Many capable people doubt whether they deserve their seat. Useful techniques:

- Separate **feelings from facts**: list the evidence of competence.
- Reframe "I am not ready" as "I will learn on the job, with support."

If anxiety is severe or persistent, encourage speaking to a counsellor or the company's EAP.

## 10. Executive presence

Executive presence is how you come across as credible and composed. It is not about being loud or looking a certain way. Build it from:

- **Clarity:** lead with the conclusion, then the reasons.
- **Composure:** pause before answering; slow your pace.
- **Concise delivery:** one-page summaries, three key points.
- **Decisiveness:** make a recommendation, not just a list of options.

## 11. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Waiting to be noticed instead of preparing a case early.
- Anchoring too low, so the final number lands below your real target.
- Opening with personal needs ("I have EMIs") instead of value and market data. Personal context is valid, but lead with contribution.
- Accepting a "no" without asking for the process, a date or a development plan.
- Treating mentors as sponsors.
- Taking on housework quietly, then resenting it.

## 12. Practice questions

1. Your manager says there is no budget for a raise this year. What are two useful next questions? *(What is the process and timeline for the next cycle, and what would I need to show? Can we agree a dated revision in writing, or non-cash options such as title or learning budget?)*
2. A colleague presents your analysis in a meeting as their own. What is a calm in-the-moment response? *(Thank them for building on the work you shared earlier and add to it, then follow up privately about future credit.)*
3. What is the difference between a mentor and a sponsor? *(A mentor advises; a sponsor advocates and spends influence for you in decisions.)*
