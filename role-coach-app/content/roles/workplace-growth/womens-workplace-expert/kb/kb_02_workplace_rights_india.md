# Workplace Rights for Women in India

*Knowledge area: Workplace rights for women in India. Audience: women professionals at any career stage, and the managers and allies who support them. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation against current statutes, rules and company policy.*

## 1. Purpose and limits

This document gives general information so that a coach can explain how the main protections work and where to go next. It is **not legal advice**. Laws change, state rules differ, and every situation has its own facts. A coach never decides whether a particular incident is or is not harassment, or whether a particular right applies. The right next step is usually to read the company policy, speak to the Internal Committee (IC), HR or a lawyer, or contact the relevant authority.

## 2. The POSH Act, 2013: overview

The Sexual Harassment of Women at Workplace (Prevention, Prohibition and Redressal) Act, 2013, commonly called the **POSH Act**, requires employers to provide a safe workplace for women and to set up a process for complaints. It applies to organised and unorganised sectors, and covers employees, contract staff, interns, trainees and visitors. The "workplace" is understood broadly and can include offsite events, business travel, and work-related online interactions. ## 3. What sexual harassment means (general terms)

The Act describes sexual harassment as unwelcome acts or behaviour, direct or implied, which can include:

- physical contact and advances
- a demand or request for sexual favours
- sexually coloured remarks
- showing pornography
- any other unwelcome physical, verbal or non-verbal conduct of a sexual nature

The Act also describes circumstances that may amount to harassment, such as implied or explicit promises of preferential treatment, threats about employment, or creating a hostile work environment. **Coaches should not tell a learner that something does or does not count.** That is for the IC and legal process. A coach can say: "What you describe sounds distressing. You can speak to your Internal Committee, HR or a lawyer, and I can walk you through how the process works."

## 4. The Internal Committee (IC)

Employers with **10 or more employees** must set up an Internal Committee at each office or administrative unit. [Verify: threshold and how it is counted.] Typical composition:

- a **Presiding Officer**, a senior woman employee
- at least **two employee members** committed to women's causes or with relevant experience
- an **external member** from an NGO or an expert on the subject
- at least **half of the members must be women**

Employers must display the IC's details, run awareness programmes and file an annual report. [Verify]

## 5. Making a complaint and the process

1. **Written complaint** to the IC. If a person cannot write, the IC must give reasonable help. Complainants can attach evidence such as messages, emails and the names of witnesses.
2. **Time limit:** generally within **3 months** of the incident, or the last incident in a series. The IC may extend by a further 3 months for reasons recorded in writing. [Verify: time limits and extensions.]
3. **Conciliation:** at the complainant's request, the IC may try to settle the matter, but not through a monetary settlement. [Verify: conciliation provisions.]
4. **Inquiry:** if no conciliation, the IC conducts an inquiry following principles of natural justice, giving both sides a hearing. The inquiry is generally to be completed within **90 days**. [Verify: inquiry and report timelines.]
5. **Report and action:** the IC submits findings, and the employer generally acts within **60 days** of the report. [Verify: timeline.] Outcomes can include disciplinary action under company rules, and compensation to the complainant.
6. **Interim relief:** during the inquiry the IC can recommend relief such as transfer or leave for the complainant. [Verify: scope of interim relief.]
7. **Appeal:** a person aggrieved by the outcome can appeal within the period set in the Act. [Verify: appeal forum and 90-day period.]

## 6. Confidentiality and protection against retaliation

The Act prohibits publishing or disclosing the identity of the complainant, respondent and witnesses, and details of the inquiry, with limited exceptions. [Verify: penalties for breach.] Retaliation, such as demotion, a bad review or exclusion because someone complained, is not acceptable under company policy and may attract legal consequences. A learner who faces retaliation should document it and speak to HR, the IC or a lawyer.

## 7. Local Committee for small workplaces

Where an employer has **fewer than 10 employees**, or the complaint is against the employer, a **Local Committee** set up by the district officer receives complaints. [Verify: how to locate the Local Committee in a given district.] Domestic workers also use the Local Committee route. [Verify]

## 8. Maternity Benefit Act, 1961 (as amended 2017)

The Act applies to establishments with **10 or more employees**. [Verify: coverage rules and state variations.] Key provisions in general terms:

- **26 weeks** of paid maternity leave for the first two children; **12 weeks** for the third child onwards. [Verify]
- Up to **8 weeks** may be taken before the expected delivery date. [Verify]
- **Adoption and commissioning mothers:** 12 weeks of leave from the date the child is handed over, for a child under three months. [Verify: age limit and eligibility.]
- **Eligibility:** generally the woman must have worked at least **80 days** in the 12 months before the expected delivery. [Verify]
- **Work from home:** after leave, the employer may permit work from home by mutual agreement, depending on the nature of work. [Verify]
- **Crèche:** establishments with **50 or more** employees must provide a crèche facility within a prescribed distance, with a set number of visits per day. [Verify]
- **No dismissal** or reduction of benefits because of maternity leave; employers must also not dismiss a woman during absence for pregnancy. [Verify: exceptions for gross misconduct.]

Miscarriage leave and a medical bonus also exist. [Verify] The ESI Act may apply instead for covered employees. [Verify]

## 9. Equal pay

India's constitutional principle of equal pay for equal work was given statutory form in the Equal Remuneration Act, 1976, now subsumed in the **Code on Wages, 2019**, which prohibits gender-based discrimination in wages and in recruitment for the same work or work of similar nature. [Verify: current commencement status of the labour codes and any state rules.] A learner who suspects a pay gap can compare her pay with transparent grade bands, ask HR for the pay-banding process, and raise concerns with HR or a lawyer.

## 10. Night shifts and conditions of work

Women working night shifts are covered by conditions in **state Shops and Establishments rules** and, where applicable, the **Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code**. These conditions generally include consent of the woman, transport, safety and surveillance, and a minimum number of women in a shift. [Verify: the exact requirements vary by state and sector.]

## 11. Worked example: knowing where to look

Meera works at a 40-person design studio in Bengaluru. She is expecting her first child in six months. She asks Maya: "How much leave do I get, and can I work from home afterwards?"

Maya's approach: explain in general terms that the Maternity Benefit Act generally provides 26 weeks for a first child [Verify], and that work from home depends on the nature of work and mutual agreement. She then points Meera to practical steps: read the leave policy in the employee handbook, ask HR for written confirmation of her entitlement and expected delivery date process, check eligibility based on days worked, and talk to her manager about the return plan. Maya does not tell her what her employer "must" do in her specific case. If HR is unhelpful, Meera can consult a lawyer or the labour department.

## 12. Finding your company's policy

- Employee handbook or HR portal: look for POSH, leave, flexible work and grievance policies.
- The POSH policy should name the IC members and contact details.
- Ask HR in writing for the latest version of a policy.
- For legal rights beyond company policy, consult a lawyer or the relevant government office.

## 13. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Giving legal conclusions ("that is definitely harassment") instead of general information and referral.
- Assuming the 3-month complaint window has passed without checking with the IC or a lawyer.
- Asking for names or company details; coaches do not need them.
- Treating maternity benefits as a favour rather than a statutory entitlement, or promising specifics for a particular employer.
- Forgetting small workplaces and the Local Committee.
