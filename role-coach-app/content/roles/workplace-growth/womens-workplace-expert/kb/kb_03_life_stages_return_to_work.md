# Life Stages, Return-to-Work and Balance

*Knowledge area: Life stages, return-to-work and balance. Audience: women professionals at any career stage, and the managers and allies who support them. Draft for coaching use; items marked [Verify] need SME confirmation.*

## 1. Why life-stage transitions need planning

Career paths are rarely a straight line. Maternity or adoption, caring for a child or an elderly parent, health, relocation or study can all change the pace of work for a while. Good planning turns these into managed transitions rather than career setbacks. The goal is a plan that the employee, the manager and the team can all trust. Statutory entitlements are covered in the workplace rights document; this one is about practical planning and conversations.

## 2. Planning maternity leave and handover

Start about two to three months before the planned leave start, once you have shared the news with your manager.

1. **Check entitlements** in the policy and with HR, including start date, leave length and any additional company benefits. [Verify: company policy vs statute.]
2. **Share the news your way.** Tell your manager first, in person or on a call, then agree how and when the team is told.
3. **Map your work.** List ongoing projects, recurring tasks, key stakeholders and deadlines in the next six to nine months.
4. **Decide the cover.** For each item choose: hand over, pause, or delegate with oversight. Name a point person.
5. **Write the handover document.** Status, next steps, contacts, files, passwords stored through approved tools, and known risks.
6. **Brief stakeholders and clients** and introduce the cover.
7. **Agree on contact boundaries** for the leave period, in writing if possible.
8. **Discuss the return** in advance: role, team, hours and a date to review.

Managers: plan the cover, do not assume what she will want, and ask, "How would you like to handle contact during leave?"

## 3. Staying connected (optional)

Some women prefer a complete break; others want light contact. Both are legitimate, and the choice belongs to the employee. Options:

- **No contact** except emergencies through HR.
- **Light touch:** a monthly team newsletter or a catch-up call.
- **Key updates only:** reorganisations, promotions or role changes that affect her.

Many companies also run **Keeping In Touch (KIT) days** or return-to-work programmes. [Verify: company programme and any pay implications.] Managers should still share information about promotions, appraisals and restructuring with a person on leave, so she is not excluded by default.

## 4. The return-to-work conversation

Hold it 4 to 6 weeks before the return date.

**Employee agenda:**

- Role and responsibilities: same role, or a changed scope?
- Hours, location and flexibility, with a trial period.
- Ramp-up: what has changed and who will brief her.
- Support: buddy, mentor, childcare arrangements and school-hour constraints.
- Goals and appraisal expectations for the year of return.

**A script:** "I am returning on 1 March and I am excited to pick up the regional account again. I would like to work three days in the office and two from home for the first three months, with a review in May. I will be fully available on core hours from 10 to 5."

## 5. Phased return

A phased return eases the transition. Typical patterns:

- Reduced hours for the first month, then step up.
- Hybrid for the first quarter.
- Projects with fewer travel demands at the start.
- A 30-60-90 day plan with the manager.

Agree the pay implications up front. A phased return may involve lower hours and sometimes pro-rata pay. [Verify: company policy.]

## 6. Managing guilt and energy

Returning can bring mixed emotions: excitement, relief, guilt about the child, worry about performance. Helpful ideas:

- **Name it.** Guilt is common and does not mean you made a wrong decision.
- **Protect energy, not just time.** Identify two or three non-negotiable recovery habits: sleep, a short walk, a weekly break.
- **Share the load at home.** Divide tasks explicitly with a partner or family; do not default to one person managing everything. Practise the conversation.
- **Lower the bar temporarily.** In the first three months, prioritise the three outcomes that matter most.
- **Use support.** Domestic help, a childcare plan with a backup, peer groups for returning mothers.
- **Know the warning signs.** Persistent low mood, anxiety, sleeplessness or exhaustion deserve professional support. Encourage speaking to a doctor or the company's EAP.

## 7. Career break re-entry

Women return after a break for many reasons: childcare, caring, relocation, health or study. Re-entry is more achievable than most people expect, and structured routes exist.

- **Returnship programmes:** paid, mid-career programmes at several large companies, typically 3 to 6 months with a path to a permanent role. [Verify: current programmes and eligibility.]
- **Contract, consulting or freelance work** to rebuild recent experience.
- **Upskilling:** short courses or certifications in tools that have changed.
- **Network:** reconnect with former colleagues, alumni groups and women's career communities. Many roles are filled through referrals.
- **Job portals and recruiters** that target career-break candidates.

## 8. Explaining a gap on your CV

Be brief, honest and forward-looking. You do not need to apologise.

- **On the CV:** list the dates and a simple line, such as "Career break: full-time caregiving, 2022 to 2025", and any projects, courses or volunteering during that time.
- **In the interview:** state the reason in one sentence, then pivot to readiness. "I took three years out to care for my family. I have since completed a data analytics certification and I am ready to return to a full-time role."
- **Do not volunteer details** you are not comfortable sharing. Keep it professional.
- **Prepare for the doubt behind the question:** "Are your skills current and are you committed?" Answer that directly.

## 9. Asking for flexible or hybrid work

Frame flexibility as a **business case**, not a favour.

1. **State the proposal clearly:** days, hours, location, start date and trial length.
2. **Show how the work gets done:** deliverables, availability windows, communication rhythm.
3. **Address the manager's concerns** before they ask: coverage, client meetings, team collaboration.
4. **Offer a trial period** and agree measures of success.
5. **Quantify** where you can: "I will keep the weekly sales report on time and be reachable between 10 and 5."
6. **Put it in writing** after agreement.

Managers: judge the output and reliability, not the hours at a desk, and apply flexibility fairly across genders.

## 10. Worked example: asking for a hybrid return

Kavita, a senior HR business partner in Gurugram, is returning after six months of leave. Her commute is 90 minutes each way and her toddler's daycare closes at 6:30 pm. She wants three days from the office and two from home.

She writes a half-page proposal: "Proposal: Tuesday to Thursday in office, Monday and Friday from home. Core hours 10 to 5, reachable until 6:30 on all days. I will keep the weekly employee-query tracker at under 24 hours' response time and attend the monthly leadership meeting in person. Trial for three months with a review on 1 June." Her manager worries about plant visits. Kavita offers to schedule plant visits on Tuesdays to Thursdays. They agree on the trial and confirm it by email.

Coaching points: specific days and hours, measurable commitments, a solution for the manager's concern, and a review date.

## 11. Caregiving for parents and others

Elder care often arrives unexpectedly and can last for years. Ask early, and plan the same way:

- Check **leave policies**, including emergency leave, special leave and unpaid leave. [Verify: company policy.]
- Share **what you need** (flexibility for appointments) and **what you can commit to**.
- Look at **practical support**: home nurses, day-care centres, and the employee benefits that may cover dependants.
- Involve siblings and family so the load is shared.
- Revisit arrangements every few months.

## 12. The manager's role

Managers shape whether a transition strengthens or weakens a career.

- Do not assume ambition drops after leave; ask the employee.
- Keep her in the loop on opportunities, and do not decide on her behalf ("She will not want the travel").
- Give a proper handover and ramp-up, and set realistic goals for the return year.
- Be fair in appraisals; avoid penalising the leave period or a phased return.
- Apply the same flexibility to fathers and other caregivers.

## 13. Common mistakes and coaching points

- Leaving handover until the last week.
- Making assumptions about contact, ambition or availability, on either side.
- Treating guilt as a signal to take on more.
- Over-apologising about a career gap.
- Asking for flexibility without a plan for deliverables.
- Skipping the written confirmation of agreed arrangements.
- Managers excluding people on leave from information on roles and promotions.

## 14. Practice questions

1. What is a good way to frame a request for hybrid work? *(As a business case: specific days, measurable deliverables, a trial period and answers to the manager's concerns.)*
2. How should you describe a three-year career break in an interview? *(Briefly and honestly in one sentence, then pivot to current skills and readiness.)*
3. What should a manager do about contact during maternity leave? *(Ask the employee her preference and record it, rather than assuming.)*
